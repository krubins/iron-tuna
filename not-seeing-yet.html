What You’re Not Seeing Yet

By Artie Kesselman · a weekly column · new edition every Tuesday through the season

A ranking is a number. This column is about what is inside the number. Same board everyone else is reading, opened up and sorted into the parts that hold and the parts that are a coin flip somebody wrote down to one decimal place.

Build My Free Auction SheetSee the whole board

What this column does. Every player on Iron Tuna’s board carries a rank, and a rank is a sum. Nobody reads the sum. So each week this column opens six of them and asks the only question the rank cannot answer on its own: which parts of this projection are likely to repeat, and which parts are the projection holding its breath?

Two numbers do most of the work. Touchdown share is the percentage of a player’s projected fantasy points that comes from touchdowns. Touchdowns are the least repeatable thing on a stat sheet; catches and yards are the most. A high share means the rank is a bet on the end zone. A low share means the rank is a bet on volume, and volume is the stickier of the two. Offense rank is where the betting market prices that player’s team in implied points per game — the size of the pie every share is a share of.

Where the numbers come from. Points are the committed 2026 projection set scored at full PPR, the same board the cheat sheet prices. Offense ranks are Iron Tuna’s market file (272 priced fixtures off the nflverse schedule release, as of September 2, 2026). Games missed come from the availability file that pro-rates every projection to the games a player can actually play. Role and injury claims are quoted from this site’s own dated camp reports, and each one is linked.

The tag on each entry is the verdict. Beats his rank means the composition of the projection is better than the rank implies. Misses his rank means it is worse. The rank is an artifact means the number is not a football opinion at all — it is arithmetic about availability, and it goes stale the day the situation moves.

The percentages are this desk’s estimate of how far off the rank is. They are not a projected stat line. If you have set your league up in the app, every one of them is restated in your budget and your scoring right under the entry.

About the byline. Artie Kesselman is the pen name this column runs under. The numbers are Iron Tuna’s and every one of them is checkable above. The opinions about them are the column’s.

Chase Brown Is the RB13 on the Fourth-Best Offense in Football, and Nobody Can Tell Me Why

Twelve running backs are ranked ahead of Chase Brown. Six of them play for offenses the betting market prices 17th or worse: Bijan Robinson (24th), Jonathan Taylor (17th), De’Von Achane (29th), Ashton Jeanty (28th), Jeremiyah Love (32nd) and Breece Hall (31st). Half the field ahead of him has been handed a small pie and told to make it look bigger. Brown has been handed the fourth-largest pie in the league.

His line is 1,038 rushing yards, 64 receptions and 10.3 touchdowns, which comes to 270.7 points. The 64 catches are the part nobody looks at, and in full PPR a catch is a point whether it happens on third and two or on a screen going nowhere. Cincinnati is priced for 26.0 points a game, fourth behind only the Rams, the Lions and the Bills. And the role is not a guess: this site’s own August 21 camp report had him at 69 to 70 percent of the Bengals’ backfield snaps through the preseason, with the carries split just as lopsidedly, and filed Samaje Perine under need-only handcuff.

So what is the case against him? He is not new. He is not hurt. He is not in a committee. He is the thirteenth name on a list, and four of the twelve above him have a ceiling set by their own scoreboard.

Touchdown share 22.8%Cincinnati implied 26.0 ppg, 4thProjected receptions 64

The tell: Chase Brown (CIN, RB). Sixty-four receptions inside the league’s fourth-best scoring environment, at a rank built for a back with neither. The volume is confirmed by snap count, not projected by hope.

Projected effect: +6% to +11% above his RB13 line

Beats his rankWRPITSep 8

Michael Pittman Jr. Catches 88 Passes and Is Ranked Behind Eighteen Men Who Catch Fewer

That is the entry. That is the whole entry. Eighty-eight receptions is the twelfth-most of any receiver on this board. Twenty-nine receivers are ranked ahead of him. Eleven of them catch more. Eighteen of them catch fewer, and they are all ranked higher anyway.

The gap is touchdowns, or rather the total absence of them: 3.9 projected receiving scores, which is 11.9 percent of his points, the lowest touchdown share of any receiver in the top thirty. Everyone else up there is getting paid partly in end zones. Pittman is being paid entirely in first downs. In a full-PPR league that is not a weakness, it is a floor, and floors are the thing you actually own in September.

Here is the uncomfortable part for the ranking. Receptions are the most repeatable input in fantasy football and touchdowns are the least. A WR30 tag built by subtracting the least repeatable input from a player who dominates the most repeatable one is not a scouting report. It is a statement about variance, printed as though it were a statement about talent. He does not have to improve. The dice have to stop being strange.

Touchdown share 11.9%, lowest in the WR top 30Receptions 88, 12th among all receiversPittsburgh implied 21.4 ppg, 25th

The tell: Michael Pittman (PIT, WR). His rank is depressed by the one input nobody can predict and inflated by nothing at all. The 88 catches are the asset, and 88 catches is WR12 volume at a WR30 price.

Projected effect: +7% to +12% above his WR30 line

The rank is an artifactRBGBSep 8

Josh Jacobs Is the RB36 Because of a Calendar, Not a Depth Chart

Nobody demoted Josh Jacobs. The board subtracted six games from him and printed the remainder.

He has been on the commissioner’s exempt list since August 30 (September 2 camp report), and this site’s availability file — the one that pro-rates every projection down to the games a player can actually play — carries him at six games out with the note “working estimate, revisit weekly.” Take his 166.8 projected points, divide by the eleven games he is projected to play, and you get 15.2 a game. That is RB15 on a per-game basis. He is listed at RB36. Nothing in between those two numbers is a football opinion.

It goes one of two ways and both are worth knowing in advance. If the exempt list lifts early, the RB36 label is stale the morning it happens, and there is no scramble for him because he has spent six weeks being ignored on somebody’s bench. If it does not lift — the first court date is November 17 — then six games was the optimistic read and he is a stash rather than a starter. The rank cannot tell you which. It quietly split the difference and rounded.

One more number worth staring at: 32.0 percent of his projected points come from touchdowns, the highest share of any back in the top forty. Whenever he returns, he returns to a goal line the market prices tenth in the league.

Per game 15.2, an RB15 paceListed RB36Touchdown share 32.0%, highest in the RB top 40

The tell: Josh Jacobs (GB, RB). The rank is arithmetic about availability, not about the player, and it moves the day the absence does. Buy the games, not the label — and know the November 17 date cuts the other way.

Projected effect: +20% to +40% above his RB36 line if the absence lands at six games or fewer

Misses his rankRBARISep 8

Jeremiyah Love Is the RB8 and Arizona Is the Market’s Worst Offense. One of Those Is Wrong.

Every offense gets a price. Arizona’s comes out 32nd of 32 at 18.8 implied points a game, and the same file has the Cardinals’ defense allowing 26.9, which is also 32nd. That is not a scoring environment. That is a hostage situation with a logo.

Love is ranked RB8 inside it. Only two other backs in the top twenty sit on an offense the market rates below 29th — Breece Hall at 31st and Quinshon Judkins at 30th — and neither is ranked anywhere near this high. The projection gets to 276.8 points honestly enough, at 1,128 rushing yards, 65 receptions and 8.8 touchdowns. Every one of those is a slice of the smallest pie in football.

Then there is the ankle. The August 21 camp report has him with a high ankle sprain from the August 13 preseason opener, ruled out of practice that week, with Mike LaFleur declining to commit to Week 1 — and the report’s own line was that a high ankle sprain is exactly the injury that drags into the regular season. James Conner, meanwhile, sits on the availability file at four games out and is first eligible in Week 5. So the workload that makes Love an RB8 has a return date printed on it. A rookie, on the worst-priced offense in the league, off the injury that lingers, with the veteran walking back through the door in October. RB8 is a great deal of confidence to hang on that stack.

Arizona implied 18.8 ppg, 32nd of 32Touchdown share 19.1%Conner first eligible Week 5

The tell: Jeremiyah Love (ARI, RB). The rank prices a locked bell-cow role on an offense the market prices last, and the two things holding that role up — a healthy ankle and an absent veteran — are both on the clock.

Projected effect: -10% to -18% below his RB8 line

Misses his rankRBBALSep 8

Derrick Henry’s Rank Needs 14 Touchdowns and Has 21 Catches to Fall Back On. There Is No Airbag.

Look at the line: 1,484 rushing yards, 14.3 touchdowns, 21 receptions. Twenty-one. Across seventeen games. That is a catch every three weeks, and on one of those weeks it was probably an accident.

Thirty-one point four percent of his projected points come from touchdowns, the highest share of any back in the top fifteen. The next-fewest receptions in that group belongs to James Cook at 36, which is fifteen more than Henry, and James Cook is not exactly running option routes out of the slot. So RB9 rests almost entirely on the one statistic that swings hardest and apologizes least. Touchdowns are not a floor. They are a guest who shows up, is magnificent, and then misses three straight Sundays without calling.

The honest counterargument deserves saying out loud: Baltimore is the fifth-best offense the market prices, at 25.8 points a game, and a good offense manufactures goal-line snaps the way a good deli manufactures a line out the door. If the touchdowns come, the rank is right and this entry is wrong. The point is not that Henry is bad. The point is that RB9 is a bet with nothing hedging it, and every back ranked above him has somewhere else to get his points from.

Touchdown share 31.4%, highest in the RB top 15Receptions 21, fewest in that group by 15Baltimore implied 25.8 ppg, 5th

The tell: Derrick Henry (BAL, RB). Nearly a third of the rank is touchdowns and there is no receiving volume underneath it, so a cold month has nothing to land on. The offense is good enough that this is a risk, not a verdict.

Projected effect: -8% to -14% below his RB9 line

Misses his rankTEATLSep 8

Kyle Pitts Is the TE8 on 80 Catches and Almost Nothing Else, Which Is Awkward, Because the Catches Are the Part in Question

Ten point four percent. That is how much of Kyle Pitts’s projection comes from touchdowns, on 3.2 projected scores — the lowest share of any tight end in the top twelve. Mark Andrews, four spots below him, gets 27.7 percent of his points from the end zone. Pitts’s entire rank is receptions and yardage. It is eighty catches in a trench coat.

Which would be fine, except for what this site reported on August 30: Pitts played 9 of 14 preseason snaps with Atlanta’s starters, against an 87.7 percent snap share last season, with Austin Hooper added this offseason and Kevin Stefanski’s two-tight-end sets on the way. That report’s advice was to keep him in the TE1 range but stop paying for last year’s full-time role, and that is the right advice. Here is the part it left on the table.

The input under threat is the only input he has. A tight end who scores can survive a snap cut, because he only needs to be on the field for the snaps that end in the end zone. A tight end whose points are eighty catches cannot survive one at all: he needs the snaps to get the targets to get the catches to get the rank, and every link in that chain just got shorter. Atlanta is also 24th in implied points, which is not the neighborhood in which you want to be running a volume business.

Touchdown share 10.4%, lowest in the TE top 12First-team preseason snaps 9 of 14 vs. an 87.7% share in 2025Atlanta implied 21.5 ppg, 24th

The tell: Kyle Pitts (ATL, TE). His rank is one input wide, and that input is the one the snap counts just called into question. There is no touchdown volume underneath it to absorb the loss.

Projected effect: -9% to -16% below his TE8 line

Each edition revisits the previous ones by name. A column that only prints new opinions and never scores the old ones is a horoscope with a snap count.
