The Pick

Market Desk·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on your lineup, your waiver claim and your draft board alike.

This week’s boardThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

Ten Running Backs Fit Inside 21 Points, So Do Not Pay Chase Brown Like He Is Ten Spots Behind Jeanty

From RB6 to RB15 the projected running back board runs 279.6 down to 258.6, a 21.0‑point plateau. Chase Brown sits in the middle of it at 270.7, and the cheapest way to cover it is to take whichever of these backs the room forgets.

Every auction has a moment when two guys bid against each other for a running back who is worth exactly the same as the one nobody is looking at. It is a fine tradition. It is also how a roster ends up with a $48 back and a $31 back who score the same on Sunday.

Here is the board, in full PPR over 17 games. Jahmyr Gibbs is 364.7, Bijan Robinson is 351.6 and Christian McCaffrey is 341.3, an average of 352.5 for the top three. Then comes a real step down: Jonathan Taylor is 315.6, 25.7 behind McCaffrey, and De’Von Achane is 292.9. That is a cliff, and you pay for the ledge you land on.

Past Achane the board stops falling and starts lying flat. Ashton Jeanty is 279.6. James Cook is 278.3, Jeremiyah Love is 276.8, Derrick Henry is 273.2, Saquon Barkley and Kenneth Walker III are both 272.6, Breece Hall is 271.3, Chase Brown is 270.7, Omarion Hampton is 261.7 and Javonte Williams is 258.6. That is ten backs inside 21.0 points, with an average of 271.5. Over 17 games it is a bit over a point a week between the sixth back and the fifteenth.

Then the floor drops again. Travis Etienne is RB16 at 245.5, 13.1 behind Williams, and Kyren Williams is 231.1 after that. So the shape of the board is three stars, a short staircase, a ten‑man shelf, and a second cliff. A shelf is where you save money, because the market will charge a different price for each of ten players who project the same.

Why Brown out of ten? Because the site’s June 25 camp report already called him Cincinnati’s clear lead back, with Samaje Perine behind him, and a clear lead is the kind of role that holds a number steady. We have no reason to argue with that call. We just notice that the market tends to price the name on the shelf, not the shelf.

The risk, out loud: a shelf is a projection, and a projection is a median. Any of those ten can break in either direction, and the 21 points between them is smaller than one bad injury. If you cannot get a shelf back at a shelf price, the move is the step above: Taylor at 315.6 is 44.9 points over Brown.

The line for your league: when ten players share 21 points, the first one you nominate is the one you do not want.

The Pick: Chase Brown (CIN, RB). Treat him as one of ten equal backs and stop bidding when his price passes the shelf’s cheapest. If the room is paying star money for the shelf, pay up once for Jonathan Taylor (IND, RB) and let the rest go.

Projected effect: +6% to +12% above his current price

Theme: positional scarcityTEARIOct 25

Trey McBride Is Worth 72.0 Points Over a Replacement Tight End, and That Is Less Than the Number Four Wide Receiver

Scarcity is points over the 12th starter at your position, not a feeling. By that measure, Trey McBride’s edge is 72.0, behind the top running back (93.4) and the top wide receiver (108.1), and close to what the fourth wide receiver gives you (75.4).

Every draft has a guy who says “tight end is so thin you have to take one early,” in the voice of a man who has just discovered fire. He is half right, which is the most dangerous amount. Tight end is thin. It just is not as thin as that voice makes it sound.

Here is the test. Take the 12th-best player at each position, the guy a 12-team league can always find, and measure how many points the best player beats him by. Jahmyr Gibbs is 364.7 against 271.3 for Breece Hall at RB12: 93.4 points. Puka Nacua is 356.0 against 247.9 for Garrett Wilson at WR12: 108.1. Josh Allen is 364.4 against 283.9 for Dak Prescott at QB12: 80.5. McBride is 241.1 against 169.1 for Mark Andrews at TE12: 72.0. All of it is full PPR over 17 games.

So the top tight end is the smallest prize of the four. The famous tight end gap is real, but it sits in absolute points, not in the points over replacement. A tight end just scores fewer of them. The wide receiver board is where the pain is: the top three average 339.6, and the 12th is 91.7 below that.

The price of being right is also smaller than it looks. Brock Bowers is 240.4, 0.7 behind McBride, so you have a pair of tight ends worth about the same and only one of you gets McBride. If the room around you chases Bowers, fine. If it chases the tight end tier as a whole, the third tight end, Tyler Warren at 210.0, is 40.9 over replacement, about half of what the third wide receiver gives you (78.3).

Why does McBride still get the pick? Because the site’s own coverage says his role is the one part of the Arizona offense a weak quarterback cannot shrink. Of the four gaps, his comes with the lowest chance that the bad part of the offense eats it. At 72.0 he is close to a WR4, and a WR4 costs a lot more.

The risk, out loud: a 72-point edge is a projection, and a tight end who misses four games hands most of it back. Scarcity also depends on your league. Start two tight ends, or a flex that takes them, and the table changes.

The line for your league: the price of a position should follow its gap over replacement, not its reputation.

The Pick: Trey McBride (ARI, TE). Pay up to about what you would pay a fourth wide receiver and not a dollar more; if the room pushes him past that, wait for Tyler Warren (IND, TE) at 210.0 and spend the difference on a wide receiver.

Projected effect: +6% to +11% above his current price

Theme: committee backfieldsRBPITOct 24

Pittsburgh’s Two Running Backs Are 5.9 Points Apart, So Stop Paying One of Them Like a Starter and the Other Like a Spare Part

Jaylen Warren projects for 191.3 PPR points and Rico Dowdle for 185.4, a gap of about 3%. Dowdle has the 975 rushing yards, and he is the cheaper way to own the same backfield.

A two-man backfield is a lot like a married couple splitting a pizza. One of them will swear they ate less, and the math says otherwise. Pittsburgh has two backs on its initial 53-man roster, and the market has already decided which one gets the bigger slice.

The site’s September 2 camp report said to pay Warren like a clear lead back and add a few dollars to Dowdle. The projection set does not see a clear lead. Warren is RB26 at 191.3 points. Dowdle is RB30 at 185.4. Four spots on the RB board, and 5.9 points over 17 games, which is about a third of a point a week. Nobody should pay a lead-back premium for a third of a point.

The reason the gap is small is that the two backs get their points from different places. Warren is the receiver: 46 catches and 320 receiving yards, against 29 catches and 211 yards for Dowdle. Dowdle is the runner: 975 rushing yards to Warren’s 791, and 5.5 rushing touchdowns to 4.5. In full PPR, Warren’s extra 17 catches are worth 17 points, and that is nearly the whole difference between them. Take the reception point out and Dowdle leads, 156.4 to 145.3.

That matches what the Play-Caller Premium said when Pittsburgh signed Dowdle for early‑down and goal‑line work next to passing‑down back Warren: a real two‑man plan, and neither back priced as a committee back yet. We agree with that read and disagree with the lead‑back label. A back who gets the early downs and the goal line does not turn into a spare part because his partner catches more passes.

The risk, out loud: a committee is a committee until one back gets hurt, and then it is a workhorse. If Warren goes down, Dowdle is the only pure back in the room and these numbers are too low. If Dowdle goes down, Warren inherits the carries and the table understates him just as much. The projection splits the risk down the middle, which is exactly why neither back should cost lead‑back money before that happens.

The line for your league: when two teammates are within 3% of each other, ask which one is cheaper. Do not ask which one is the starter.

The Pick: Rico Dowdle (PIT, RB). Bid him as an equal partner in the Pittsburgh backfield, because the projection puts him 5.9 points behind Warren and ahead of him in standard scoring. If Warren has already gone for a lead‑back price, let him go and take Jaylen Warren (PIT, RB) only in a PPR league at a real discount.

Projected effect: +8% to +14% above his current price
