Nate Vega·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on your lineup, your waiver claim and your draft board alike.

This week’s boardThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

A Coin Flip Is Not a Starter: Jonathon Brooks Costs Less Than Chuba Hubbard and Projects Nearly the Same

Carolina’s two backs are projected 1.6 points apart, 171.5 for Chuba Hubbard and 169.9 for Jonathon Brooks. When a backfield splits like that, you are bidding on one pie, and you should only pay for one slice of it.

There is a kind of auction bidder who sees “starting running back” on a name card and treats it like a title of nobility. Meanwhile the other back on the same team, who the projection says will do almost the same job, sits on the board at a discount and nobody lifts a paddle. It is the fantasy version of paying full price for the left shoe.

The test is simple. In the projection set, look at the teams that put two backs in the top 45 and see how far apart the two are. Four of them are within six points: New England (Rhamondre Stevenson 202.0, TreVeyon Henderson 196.8), Pittsburgh (Jaylen Warren 191.3, Rico Dowdle 185.4), Washington (Rachaad White 163.7, Jacory Croskey‑Merritt 158.3) and Carolina. Carolina is the tightest of the four, at 1.6 points. Compare that to Detroit, where Jahmyr Gibbs is 364.7 and the next back is a rounding error, or Baltimore, where Derrick Henry leads Justice Hill by 159.2.

That gap is the whole story. Every back in the top 12 of the position is the unquestioned lead on his team. Once you leave that group, the “starter” label stops meaning one man, and the points sit in a pie that two players share. Carolina’s pie is 341.4 points. Hubbard gets 171.5 of it on 709 rushing yards and 41 catches. Brooks gets 169.9 on 810 rushing yards, 4.3 rushing touchdowns and 33 catches. Brooks carries more yardage and more touchdowns; Hubbard has the extra catches. That is a split, not a depth chart.

Now the disagreement, out loud. The site’s own Auction Watch has said two things about Carolina. On August 22 it called Brooks the clear lead back, playing nearly every snap with the starters. On September 1 it said to pay Hubbard like a true starter and treat Brooks as a handcuff, with Hubbard expected back from a hamstring strain for the opener. This column leans toward the first read, and here is the reason: the projection set does not agree that Hubbard is the starter. It has the two backs a hair apart, with Brooks ahead on rushing yards. If you pay starter money for Hubbard and handcuff money for Brooks, you are paying a premium for a gap the numbers do not show.

The mechanism is price, not talent. A bidder pays for the label, so the label back goes for more and the other back goes for less, even when the points are equal. The market is rounding a 1.6‑point gap up to a role gap. You do not have to bet on who is better. You only have to refuse to pay the difference.

The risk is the obvious one. If Hubbard is healthy and the coaches simply prefer him in the red zone, the split tilts his way and Brooks’s 4.3 touchdowns shrink. Brooks has also been through a long recovery, and a back with that history is one bad Sunday from a smaller number. Both are real. They are why this is a price call and not a prediction: take Brooks at a handcuff’s price, and do not chase him up to Hubbard’s.

The line for your league: when two backs split one pie, buy the cheaper slice. The pie does not care which name is on the card.

The Pick: Jonathon Brooks (CAR, RB). Bid him as RB35 and stop if the room pushes him toward Hubbard’s number. If you already hold Chuba Hubbard (CAR, RB) at a starter’s price, you have already paid for the label; the 1.6 points are the same whichever of them you own.

Projected effect: +6% to +11% above his current price

Theme: touchdown regressionWRNYJOct 8

Touchdowns Are Weather: Garrett Wilson Is Owed Two, and Davante Adams Is Overdrawn

The top 36 receivers score a touchdown every 160 yards. Davante Adams is projected to score one every 96. Garrett Wilson is projected to score one every 225. Both lines bend back toward the middle.

A receiving yard is a skill. A receiving touchdown is a skill that has to wait for the offense to reach the three‑yard line, wait for the play call, wait for the safety to blink. Every analyst knows this, and every auction room forgets it the moment a name has a big number next to the touchdown column. Touchdowns are weather. You can dress for it, but you should not pay for it in advance.

So here is the test, and anyone can run it on their own sheet. Take the top 36 receivers in the projection set by full PPR points. Together they carry 40,039 receiving yards and 250.5 receiving touchdowns, which works out to 0.63 touchdowns per 100 yards. That is the room’s normal. Now ask who sits far from it.

Davante Adams (LAR) is the loud answer. He is WR18 at 232.4 points, on 1,016 yards, and he is projected for 10.6 touchdowns. That is 1.04 per 100 yards, about two thirds above the normal. Put him at the normal rate and he scores 6.4 touchdowns, which is 25.5 points fewer, and he lands at about 206.9. That is a WR27 outcome priced like a WR18, in an offense where Puka Nacua soaks up the first look and where the red‑zone targets are shared. Nobody is saying he is finished. We are saying the touchdown line is the part of his season that is borrowed.

Now the other side of the ledger. Garrett Wilson (NYJ) is WR12 at 247.9 points on 1,172 yards, more yardage than Rashee Rice or Chris Olave, and he is projected for only 5.2 touchdowns, a rate of 0.44 per 100. At the normal rate he scores 7.3, which is 12.8 points more, and he lands at about 260.7. That would put him ahead of Rice (260.0) and behind only Drake London (269.0) among the receivers who are not in the top six. Same player, same targets, same catches. The only change is that the end zone treats him like everyone else.

A word on the site’s own calls, because this column should not quietly contradict them. The July 3 Auction Watch said to pay up for a healthy Wilson, and the camp reports have him a full participant and on track for Week 1. This is the same call with an extra reason: the price you pay for his health is already fair, and the touchdown correction is the part nobody is charging you for.

The risk, out loud: regression is a lean, not a law. The Jets can be a bad offense that never gets to the goal line, in which case 5.2 was generous and he sits where the projection says. Adams, for his part, has been a touchdown machine for a decade, and a player can hold a rate above the normal for years. The entry assumes the average, and the average is a good bet, not a sure thing.

The line to take to your league: yards are what a receiver is, touchdowns are what happened to him. Bid on the first and rent the second.

The Pick: Garrett Wilson (NYJ, WR). Bid him as a WR8 and treat the first touchdown rebound as free. On the other side, let Davante Adams (LAR, WR) go to whoever wants to pay for 10.6 touchdowns, and do not cross the WR18 price for him. If the room starts bidding on Adams by the touchdown, you are the seller.

Projected effect: +5% to +9% above his current price

Theme: tier cliffsRBDALOct 7

Running Back Has a Plateau, Not a Ladder, and Javonte Williams Is the Cheap Seat on It

Nine running backs, RB7 through RB15, sit inside 19.7 PPR points of each other. Past that plateau the board drops 13.1 points to the next back. Pay for the cliff edge, not for the rung you happen to like.

An auction room treats a ranking like a ladder: every rung is a little better than the one below it, so every rung gets a little more money. That is a lovely picture and it is not how running backs are shaped. The board is more like a staircase built by someone who got bored. A few big steps, a long flat landing, then a hole.

Start at the top. Jahmyr Gibbs (DET) projects for 364.7 PPR points, Bijan Robinson (ATL) for 351.6 and Christian McCaffrey (SF) for 341.3. Jonathan Taylor (IND) is next at 315.6, a 25.7‑point drop from McCaffrey, and Achane (MIA) is 22.7 behind Taylor at 292.9. Those are real steps. If you want to pay for a cliff, pay for the one between the first four and everybody else.

Now the landing. James Cook (BUF) is RB7 at 278.3. Jeremiyah Love, Derrick Henry, Saquon Barkley, Kenneth Walker III, Breece Hall, Chase Brown and Omarion Hampton follow, and the ninth back on the landing is Javonte Williams (DAL) at 258.6. That is the whole plateau: 19.7 points across nine players, about 1.2 points a game from the top of it to the bottom. Walker and Barkley are tied at 272.6. No room should be putting a multi‑dollar gap between players a rounding error apart.

And then the hole. The next back, Travis Etienne (NO), is at 245.5, which is 13.1 points under Williams. After him it is Kyren Williams (LAR) at 231.1, then Cam Skattebo and Quinshon Judkins at 226.5 and 226.4. So the plateau is the last neighborhood where a back costs you no real production. Fall off the edge and you give up 13 points the instant you go one name down.

So why Williams, the last name on the landing? Because he is the one the room is least likely to price like a member of it. He is the bottom of the group on points, so he draws the smallest bid, and the cost of being wrong about the order is 1.2 points a week. And the role is not in doubt. The September 1 camp report says Jaydon Blue’s exit leaves him with no real competition in Dallas, and the site’s call that day was to pay full workhorse price for him. This column does not argue with it. It argues that “full workhorse price” should be the bottom of the plateau’s price, not the top.

The risk, out loud: his line leans on the end zone. He is projected for 11.1 rushing touchdowns, which is 66.6 of his 258.6 points, against only 37 catches and 218 receiving yards. Touchdowns are the part of a season that comes and goes. A back like that can finish 20 points under the projection and nobody will be surprised, and if he does he falls into the hole with Etienne. Receiving backs like Love, with 65 catches, and Hall, with 52, have a sturdier floor for the same money.

The line to take to your league: running back is not a ladder, it is a landing, and the only rung worth a premium is the last one before the hole.

The Pick: Javonte Williams (DAL, RB), and bid him as the cheapest back on the plateau, never above where the room puts Barkley or Walker. If somebody bids past Cook’s number on any of the nine, let them; you will get the same points for less from the last chair. Do not chase a back off the edge for the sake of a name.

Projected effect: +6% to +11% above his current price
