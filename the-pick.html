How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

Washington’s Backfield Isn’t Waiting on an Injury to Split the Points. It Already Has.

Rachaad White projects for 163.7 points to Jacory Croskey‑Merritt’s 158.3 — a 3.3% gap that has nothing to do with anyone getting hurt. White is just playing a different position than the depth chart says he is.

Every draft room has someone who treats “backup running back” as one undifferentiated species — all of them the same guy in a different jersey, sitting on the bench waiting for somebody’s hamstring to fail so he can matter. That is a fine model for the handcuffs this column already flagged in Pittsburgh and Carolina, where the backup’s whole value proposition is the starter falling down. It is the wrong model for Washington. Rachaad White already has a job. The job pays as well as the starter’s.

Jacory Croskey‑Merritt projects for 158.3 points on 848 rushing yards and 7.4 touchdowns — the early‑down, goal‑line workload a seventh‑round rookie earns after rushing for 805 yards and 8 scores as a first‑year pro. Rachaad White projects for 163.7 — more than Croskey‑Merritt, not less — on a completely different diet: 568 rushing yards, but 40 catches for 267 yards, more than double Croskey‑Merritt’s 16 receptions. That is not two backs splitting one job three days a week each. That is two backs each running a full route to a different end zone.

This desk called the shape of it back on September 5: Dan Quinn’s backfields have kept a receiving lane open next to the lead back for a decade, the same way Devonta Freeman kept catching passes in Atlanta even after the rushing job was fully his. Washington’s own camp reporting has Croskey‑Merritt locked in as the early‑down and goal‑line back, with White — who opened camp working back from a hamstring strain — ticketed for what the desk called a “complementary role.” That is exactly the language a market prices as a handcuff. The board disagrees, because the board counts catches, and White’s catches were never contingent on Croskey‑Merritt missing a snap.

Washington is not the only backfield doing this, and it is worth seeing the whole spectrum to trust the shape. Tampa Bay splits the same way through a different door: Kenneth Gainwell projects for 190.0 points to Bucky Irving’s 205.5, a 7.5% gap, because Gainwell is out‑targeting Irving on passing downs (59 catches to 37) even though Irving is the clear rushing starter. Now look at the other end of the spectrum, where a backfield does not split at all: Bijan Robinson clears Brian Robinson Jr. by 257.7 points, 73.3% of Bijan’s own total. That is what an actual workhorse does to the man behind him — nothing. Robinson Jr. is not a discounted half of a committee. He is an insurance policy that never pays out unless Bijan is off the field entirely.

Say the risk out loud. White’s role is safer than most complementary backs’, but it is not armor‑plated. If Washington leans run‑heavy in games it is already winning — the exact script that inflates Croskey‑Merritt’s goal‑line touchdown count — White’s catch‑based floor thins out at precisely the moment the box score looks best for the other guy. And unlike a Pittsburgh‑ or Carolina‑style handcuff, an injury to Croskey‑Merritt would not hand White a job to inherit. He would just be adding rushing volume on top of the receiving role he is already keeping, health notwithstanding.

The Pick: Rachaad White (WAS, RB), priced like a committee back who needs Croskey‑Merritt to get hurt to matter. He does not. His own role already prices out within 3.3% of Washington’s “starter,” independent of anybody’s health.

Projected effect: +9% to +14% above a standard committee-backup price

There Are Two Elite Tight Ends in Fantasy Football This Year. Everyone Else Is Noise.

Brock Bowers and Trey McBride sit 30.4 points clear of the next tight end on Iron Tuna’s board. After that cliff, the next ten names are separated by 40.9 points combined — less than the single step it takes to fall off the top tier.

Every draft room has the guy who waits on tight end. He says it with the confidence of someone who read a strategy column once: running back and receiver are deep, so why spend early money on a position you can stream in October? He is right about running back and receiver. He is about to be wrong about tight end, because this year the position isn’t a depth chart with a soft slope down it. It is a velvet rope with two names on the list and a long, flat line behind it.

Here is the rope. Brock Bowers projects for 240.4 points in full PPR over 17 games, Trey McBride for 241.1 — a rounding error between them. Then the floor drops out. Tyler Warren, the TE3, projects for 210.0 points. That is a 30.4‑point gap in one step, about 1.8 points a week, which is the gap between a tight end you start blind every Sunday and one you are checking the injury report on before you trust him in a good matchup.

That gap isn’t noise in the model, it is usage. McBride and Bowers are not splitting a route tree with two or three other pass‑catchers the way most tight ends do — they are their offense’s answer key. Arizona benched Kyler Murray for Jacoby Brissett this offseason, a real downgrade for everyone else in that building, but this desk’s own read on McBride is that his catch radius makes him “the safety‑valve target no matter who is throwing him the ball,” the one role a bad quarterback cannot shrink. Vegas is a smaller-name situation, but the shape is identical: the desk flagged Klint Kubiak’s arrival as a mild positive for Bowers specifically because the Raiders need an easy, high‑floor answer over the middle of the field. Two different quarterback situations, one identical conclusion — feed the tight end no matter what else on the roster is broken.

Now look at what happens after the cliff. Warren’s 210.0 points at TE3 lead into Loveland (204.9), Fannin (188.5), LaPorta (188.3) and Kittle (187.8), all the way down to Mark Andrews at TE12, 169.1. That is 40.9 points spread across ten ranks — smaller than the 30.4‑point gap Bowers and McBride open up in one step above them. Compare that to wide receiver, where Puka Nacua’s 356.0 points fall to Ja’Marr Chase’s 336.6 and keep sliding in real, gradual steps rank by rank. Tight end does not have a slope. It has a ledge, and then a parking lot.

Say the risk out loud. Bowers’s edge over McBride is built almost entirely on touchdowns — 6.6 modeled receiving scores against McBride’s 5.3, on fewer catches (99 to 108) and fewer yards (999 to 1,023). Touchdowns are the least stable number in any projection, so if Las Vegas’s red‑zone offense underperforms, Bowers is the one who gives back the separation, not McBride. And because the tier below is a parking lot, not a staircase, an injury to either top‑two tight end drops you straight into a cluster of nine other names within two points a game of each other — there is no quiet TE3 waiting to absorb the loss.

The Pick: Brock Bowers (LV, TE), at a price at or above what the market charges for a “TE2 discount” behind McBride — his 6.6 modeled receiving touchdowns are the difference‑maker in a tier this thin, and a true TE1 profile in a two‑man tier deserves a TE1 price, not a hedge a rank behind. If you miss both, do not reach into the TE3‑to‑TE12 cluster on draft night; those ten names are separated by 40.9 points combined, so waiting to stream one off the wire in October costs you almost nothing you did not already give up by missing the top two.

Projected effect: +8% to +14% above a typical TE2 discount to McBride

Michael Pittman and DK Metcalf Cost the Same in Full PPR. Standard Scoring Prices Them a Tier Apart.

Pittsburgh’s two starting receivers sit a single point apart on Iron Tuna’s full‑PPR board — 196.5 to 195.6. Take the reception credit away and the gap becomes 22.1 points and 14 ranks, because one of them built his number on catches and the other built his on yards and touchdowns.

Every league has the same argument in Week 1 of the group chat: somebody says “we’re PPR, so it doesn’t matter,” as if typing the letters settles who the better receiver is. It does not settle anything. It just means one specific stat — the catch itself, regardless of what it gains — is worth exactly one point in your league and worth nothing at all in your buddy’s. Most years that is a rounding error. This year, on one roster, it is a full receiver tier.

Pittsburgh’s receiver room is the cleanest version of this argument the board has anywhere: same offense, same quarterback, same play‑caller, and the two starters land almost exactly on top of each other in full PPR. Michael Pittman projects for 196.5 points, good for WR30. DK Metcalf projects for 195.6, good for WR31. One point and one rank separate them — the board is telling you, in full PPR, to treat them as the same player. Strip receptions out of the formula and score it standard instead, and Metcalf jumps to WR28 at 130.6 points while Pittman falls to WR42 at 108.5. Two guys who cost the same a minute ago are now a 22.1‑point, 14‑rank apart, and neither one of them changed a single game log to get there. The scoring format did all of it.

Look at how each one actually built his full‑PPR total and the swing stops being a mystery. Pittman’s line is 88 catches for 863 yards and 3.9 touchdowns — 9.8 yards a catch, a score roughly once every 23 grabs. Metcalf’s line is 65 catches for 944 yards and 6.2 touchdowns — 14.5 yards a catch, a score roughly once every 10‑and‑a‑half. Pittman’s extra 23 catches over Metcalf are worth 23 PPR points by definition, which is nearly the entire gap between them in full PPR. Take receptions off the table and what is left is real yardage and real scoring — and that is Metcalf’s game, not his teammate’s. Our own camp desk called this shape before either man caught a pass in Pittsburgh: the June trade for Pittman was flagged as direct target competition for Metcalf, and an August 29 report still had Metcalf and Pittman running as Pittsburgh’s top two with Roman Wilson a clear third. The target competition happened exactly as advertised — it just landed as more low‑value catches for Pittman rather than fewer catches for Metcalf, which is the one outcome a raw PPR ranking cannot tell you apart from any other.

Pittsburgh is the sharpest example, not the only one. Pull every wide receiver with at least 100 full‑PPR points and the position keeps 64.2% of its value on average once catches stop being worth a point. Pittman keeps only 55.2% — a worse haircut than the position’s own average — while Metcalf keeps 66.8%, better than average. The same catch‑heavy pattern shows up leaguewide: Wan’Dale Robinson and Josh Downs both rank inside the top 50 in full PPR and fall completely out of the WR5 range in standard. Rome Odunze runs the other direction — fewer catches, more yards and touchdowns per catch — and actually climbs the board when the reception credit disappears.

Say the risk out loud both ways. Pittman’s catch volume is real and it is not worthless in a true PPR league — 88 grabs is a genuine floor, and if Pittsburgh’s offense settles into feeding him more of the red zone as the season goes on, some of that touchdown gap closes on its own. This is not an argument to bench him in full PPR. It is an argument that his price should move a lot more than Metcalf’s does when the format changes, and right now the market treats them like the same asset everywhere. Metcalf carries a different risk entirely: he was out from August 8 until early September with an undisclosed injury before returning to full practice and telling reporters he felt 100 percent ahead of the opener. A setback there hurts him in any scoring format, PPR or not — that risk has nothing to do with how a catch gets counted.

The Pick: In any league that isn’t full PPR, discount Michael Pittman (PIT, WR) below his full‑PPR consensus price — his value is almost entirely catch count, and standard and half‑PPR formats do not pay for catches the way this board’s default does. Draft DK Metcalf (PIT, WR) at his current price in those same leagues; his profile holds up across scoring formats in a way his own teammate’s does not.

Projected effect: -15% to -20% off his price outside full-PPR leagues
