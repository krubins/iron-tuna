The Pick

The Pick

Market Desk·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on your lineup, your waiver claim and your draft board alike.

This week’s boardThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

The column
Theme: target concentrationWRSEAOct 20

Nico Collins Owns 42.5% of Houston’s Passing Game, and Jaxon Smith‑Njigba Owns 41.8% of a Pie That Is 922 Yards Bigger

Target hogs are only worth their share if the pie is worth eating. Seattle’s receiver projects for 1,569 yards at WR3; Houston’s, with nearly the same slice, projects for 1,203 at WR11.

Every league has a friend who says his receiver “gets all the targets.” This is said the way a man says his sandwich is the biggest at the picnic. It may be true. It is also possible that the picnic is two sandwiches and a bag of pretzels.

So measure the picnic. Take the projection set, add up the receiving yards of every back, tight end and receiver on a team, and ask what share belongs to the top man. Only teams with a full group of pass‑catchers in the pool count. On that list the top two shares are almost the same: Nico Collins at 42.5% of Houston and Jaxon Smith‑Njigba at 41.8% of Seattle. Ja’Marr Chase (39.5% of Cincinnati) and Puka Nacua (39.3% of the Rams) are next.

Now the size of the pie. Houston’s whole group projects for 2,831 receiving yards, the smallest of any team we can measure. Seattle’s is 3,753, which is 922 yards more. Collins gets 1,203 of his and Smith‑Njigba gets 1,569 of his, a gap of 366 yards on nearly identical slices. In full PPR over 17 games that is 326.2 points at WR3 against 247.9 at WR11, a difference of 78.3.

The mechanism is that share is a ratio and ratios hide the denominator. Collins’s next best pass‑catcher is Dalton Schultz at 552 yards; the rest of the room is Tank Dell at 317 and Xavier Hutchinson at 230. Nobody is taking targets from Collins, because there is hardly anyone to take them from, and that is exactly why the total is low. Smith‑Njigba has company, with Rashid Shaheed at 647 and Cooper Kupp at 472, and he still takes 117 catches. That is the better sign: a man who is the first read when there are other good reads is a man the quarterback trusts.

The site has already priced this side of the argument. Its Auction Watch told you to pay up for Smith‑Njigba and called his concentration what made him one of the safest receivers on the board. Nothing in the set disagrees, and the 42.5% on the other side of the table is not a reason to chase Collins. It is a reason to ask what he would do in a different pie.

The risk, out loud: a big share is also a single point of failure. If Smith‑Njigba misses time, his 1,569 yards do not vanish, they go to Shaheed and Kupp, and your roster is the part that goes away. And Collins at WR11 is a fair price for a back‑end WR1. If the room discounts him heavily for the small Houston pie, that can be a real bargain. Do not pay Smith‑Njigba money for him, and do not pay a discount for Smith‑Njigba either.

The line for your league: do not ask who gets the targets, ask how many targets there are.

The Pick: Jaxon Smith‑Njigba (SEA, WR). Treat him as the top‑three receiver he is and bid up to his price without flinching. The other side of the table is Nico Collins (HOU, WR), whose 42.5% share is real and whose 2,831‑yard offense is why it only buys WR11.

Projected effect: +4% to +8% above his current price

Theme: committee backfieldsRBNEOct 19

New England’s Backfield Is 398.8 Points Split Two Ways, and TreVeyon Henderson Is the Half the Room Will Underbid

Rhamondre Stevenson and TreVeyon Henderson are projected 5.2 points apart at RB23 and RB24. Together they total 398.8, more than Jahmyr Gibbs at 364.7, and Henderson is the one with the rushing yards.

A committee backfield is a restaurant with one very good steak and two people holding forks. Somebody is going to be hungry, and the menu does not say who. Fantasy rooms deal with this by pretending the problem is solved: they pick the name they recognize, pay him a starter’s price, and let the other guy go for the price of a parking pass.

So measure the plate before you argue about the forks. Take full PPR over 17 games and add up what each backfield is projected to produce. New England’s two backs total 398.8 points. That is 34.1 more than the best single back in the pool, Gibbs at 364.7, and it is spread over two roster spots instead of one.

Now the split. Rhamondre Stevenson projects for 202.0 points at RB23, on 740 rushing yards, 42 catches and 9.0 touchdowns. TreVeyon Henderson projects for 196.8 at RB24, on 837 rushing yards, 39 catches and 8.1 touchdowns. The gap between them is 5.2 points, about a third of a point a game, and the set gives Henderson 97 more rushing yards on the ground. Stevenson is ahead only because he catches three more passes and falls into 0.9 more scores.

The mechanism is that a committee prices the name, not the work. Two backs 5.2 points apart are, for planning purposes, the same player in two jerseys, and the room will pay for the one it has heard of longer. Meanwhile the yards point the other way. A back who makes 837 rushing yards out of a shared load is a back whose carries are working, and carries that work are the ones a coach gives more of.

The site has already made this call. The Play‑Caller column says Henderson’s snap share started climbing late in camp, the same kind of shift that preceded Henry taking over the Titans’ backfield outright. This entry agrees, and adds a reason from the pool: if the carries tilt his way, the projection is the floor of the case, not the ceiling.

The risk, out loud: the site’s September 8 report had Henderson missing practice with an ankle injury and trending toward a Week 1 scratch, so check where he is before you bid. A split backfield also caps both men. If the snaps stay even, you own half a plate, and 196.8 is the whole job, not a starter’s 270. Do not pay Stevenson money for him. Pay RB24 money, and treat the climb as free.

The line for your league: when the backfield is a committee, you are buying the plate, so do not overpay for the fork.

The Pick: TreVeyon Henderson (NE, RB). Bid him as the RB24 he is, not the cheap half of a pair, and take the carries he is gaining as the upside. If you want the safer side of the split, Rhamondre Stevenson (NE, RB) at 202.0 is the one the room will pay a premium for.

Projected effect: +5% to +9% above his current price

Theme: touchdown regressionRBMIAOct 18

Derrick Henry Is a Touchdown Count in a Trench Coat: 31.4% of His Points Are Scores, and De’Von Achane Needs Only 16.4%

Touchdowns are the least repeatable points in fantasy, and the projection set prices Derrick Henry on 14.3 of them. De’Von Achane projects 19.7 points higher at RB5 while leaning on scores for just 16.4% of his total.

Every league has a guy who says he “just likes to score touchdowns.” Great. So does everyone. The touchdown is the one stat in football that arrives by committee, by coin flip and occasionally by a quarterback sneak that makes your running back look like a man watching his own parade. You can hold the league’s best goal‑line back and still lose a week because the other team’s kicker had a good feeling.

So test the sheet for it. Take full PPR over 17 games, count the points that come from rushing and receiving scores at six apiece, and ask what share of each back’s total is built on them. The rest, yards and catches, is the part that travels from week to week. The first number is the one that regresses.

Derrick Henry is the clean case. He projects for 273.2 points at RB9, with 13.1 rushing and 1.2 receiving touchdowns. That is 14.3 scores, worth 85.8 points, or 31.4% of everything he does. Strip them out and he has 187.4 points left.

De’Von Achane projects for 292.9 at RB5 on 1,308 rushing yards, 65 catches and 511 receiving yards. He is projected for only eight touchdowns, five on the ground and three through the air, worth 48.0 points, or 16.4%. Take those away and he still has 244.9, which is 57.5 points more than Henry’s leftover. Kyren Williams sits near Henry’s end of the scale at 30.6% on 11.8 touchdowns, and he is RB17 at 231.1.

The mechanism is that yards and catches follow a role, and a role is sticky. Achane’s line is built on 65 receptions on top of the carries, a floor that shows up even in a week with no trip to the end zone. Henry’s is built on a goal‑line job, and goal‑line jobs pay beautifully right up until a quarterback keeps the ball or the offense stalls at the 3. Anything that depends on 14 scores has a wide ledger of ways to come in at 9.

That is what the column wants you to price. The site’s camp reporting says Achane’s workload is not in danger, and nothing in the set argues. So when the room pays Henry the “proven scorer” premium and treats Achane as the fragile one, you are being charged for the least repeatable part of the board. Bid on the back whose points mostly come from things that happen every Sunday.

The risk, out loud: Henry’s 187.4 non‑touchdown points are not nothing, and a 273.2 back is a fine one. Achane is projected for only 8 scores, and a small touchdown count has its own variance, in either direction. If your league pays six for a passing touchdown or bonuses for 100 yards, shares shift. And if your room already prices Achane at a star’s number, the edge is gone, so do not chase him there.

The line for your league: touchdowns are the tip, not the bill.

The Pick: De’Von Achane (MIA, RB). Bid on him as a top‑five back and treat any discount against Henry as free. If you would rather play the other side, Derrick Henry (BAL, RB) is the one to let someone else overpay for.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% above his current price
