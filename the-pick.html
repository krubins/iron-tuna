The Pick

The Pick

Market Desk·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on your lineup, your waiver claim and your draft board alike.

This week’s boardThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

Theme: touchdown regressionRBLARNov 2

Kyren Williams and Davante Adams Both Score Touchdowns at a Pace the Rams Cannot Afford to Keep, So Sell the Scoring, Not the Yards

Kyren Williams is projected for 11.8 touchdowns on 1,295 yards and Davante Adams for 10.6 on 1,016. At the rate the top 36 backs and receivers actually score, that is about 18.7 points of Williams and 25.2 points of Adams that the yardage does not explain.

A touchdown is the most exciting thing in football and the least repeatable. A man can gain 80 yards and be tackled at the one, and the other guy walks in from the one and gets the credit, the six points and the spike. Over a season it mostly evens out. Fantasy leagues, famously, do not mostly even out. They pay a bidder in March for what he did in December.

Here is the benchmark. Among this site’s top 36 running backs, touchdowns arrive at 0.67 per 100 scrimmage yards. Among the top 36 receivers, it is 0.63. Those are the going rates. Anyone far above them is either a genuine goal‑line force or a player the projection is being generous to, and the generous cases are the ones worth a second look.

Two Rams stand out. Davante Adams is the highest in the whole receiver group at 1.04 touchdowns per 100 yards, with 10.6 scores on 1,016 yards. Kyren Williams is third among the backs at 0.91, with 11.8 scores on 1,295 yards. Give each the league‑typical rate for his own yardage and Adams drops from 10.6 touchdowns to 6.4, Williams from 11.8 to 8.7. That takes Adams from 232.4 to 207.2 points, about 11% less, and Williams from 231.1 to 212.4, about 8% less. Full PPR, 17 games, nothing else changed.

Now the mechanism, because a rate is not a reason. Los Angeles has Puka Nacua, projected for 1,590 receiving yards and 9.7 receiving touchdowns, and Matthew Stafford throwing 34.9 touchdown passes. There are only so many scores to go around, and the Rams’ offense is already spreading them across three stars. On the ground, the site’s own August 31 Auction Watch reported that Blake Corum is in line for close to a 50‑50 split of the carries with Williams, after taking a near‑even share down the stretch last year. A back who shares the work does not usually keep the goal‑line job all to himself.

The risk, out loud: league‑average is a floor on the argument, not a law. Some players do score above the going rate year after year, usually because a team trusts them at the goal line, and Stafford throwing nearly 35 scores gives Adams more chances than most receivers will ever see. Henry is on the table to show that the rule is not just about the Rams: he runs hot too, at 0.84. But his 1,694 yards are a different foundation than 1,295, and his 255.5 would still be an elite back. The fade is a price argument, not a prediction that either Ram falls apart.

Also, nothing here says to avoid the yards. Williams’ 1,295 and Adams’ 1,016 are real. The mistake is paying for the 18.7 and the 25.2 as if they were as dependable as the rest.

The Pick: Davante Adams (LAR, WR). Price him as a receiver of about 207 points, not 232, and let someone else pay for the extra four touchdowns. Kyren Williams (LAR, RB) gets the same treatment at about 212, especially with Corum sharing the carries. If either one is cheap enough to cover the lower number, buy.

Projected effect: -8% to -11% below his current price

Theme: scoring settingsRBBALNov 1

Derrick Henry Is RB9 in PPR and RB5 When Receptions Pay Nothing, So Check Your League’s Settings Before You Check His Price

Take the point per catch away and Derrick Henry climbs four spots, while Ashton Jeanty and Jeremiyah Love each fall six or more. The same player pool gives two different boards, and plenty of rooms price off only one of them.

Somewhere right now a league commissioner is explaining that “it’s basically standard, we just give a little something for catches.” That sentence has cost people more money than any injury. A point per reception is not a little something. It is the whole plot, and the running backs who live on it are not the same men as the running backs who live on carries.

Start with the cleanest case. This site projects Derrick Henry for 1,484 rushing yards, 13.1 rushing touchdowns and just 21 catches. Ashton Jeanty gets 65 catches. Jeremiyah Love gets 65 catches. De’Von Achane gets 65 catches. Henry’s receptions are worth 21 points in a full PPR league. Theirs are worth 65. That 44‑point difference is invisible if you only read a PPR list, and it is the entire argument.

In full PPR over 17 games, the order is Achane at 292.9 (RB5), Jeanty at 279.6 (RB6), Love at 276.8 (RB8) and Henry at 273.2 (RB9). Henry sits behind all three. Zero out the reception point and the board flips: Henry scores 252.2 and is RB5, Achane is 227.9 and RB8, Jeanty is 214.6 and RB12, and Love is 211.8 and RB13. Henry goes from 19.7 points behind Achane to 24.3 points ahead of him, and from 6.4 behind Jeanty to 37.6 ahead.

The mechanism is boring, which is why it keeps working. A catch is a cheap point that a pass‑catching back collects in bulk and a between‑the‑tackles back barely sees. Baltimore’s offense still runs through Lamar Jackson’s legs and Henry’s carries, and the site’s own Play‑Caller column calls Henry the safest bell‑cow complement in the league. That is a rushing story, and a rushing story is exactly what a no‑PPR league pays for.

The risk, out loud: this only works if your league really does pay little or nothing for catches. In a full‑PPR room the table above is just a list of back‑catchers beating a grinder, and you should not pay a dollar extra for Henry. Henry also carries a touchdown load, 13.1 rushing scores, that no back repeats forever, and a thirty‑something runner is always one bad hit from a different forecast. The edge is a settings edge, not a talent edge.

The habit worth stealing is cheap. Before the draft, open your league’s scoring page, find the line that says what a reception is worth, and write it on your hand. Then look at the market price of the 65‑catch backs and the 21‑catch backs. If they are priced like the settings are PPR and yours are not, one side of that ledger is a gift.

The Pick: Derrick Henry (BAL, RB). In a standard or half‑point league, treat him as a top‑five back and bid to that price. In full PPR, leave him alone and let someone else pay for the carries. The cautionary mirror is Ashton Jeanty (LV, RB): in a no‑reception league he is RB12, so do not pay RB6 money for him.

Projected effect: +6% to +11% above his current price in leagues that pay little or nothing for receptions

Theme: tier cliffsWRDALOct 31

CeeDee Lamb Is the Last Wide Receiver Before the Cliff, and George Pickens Is Standing in the Parking Lot

Wide receiver has a 23.7‑point cliff between WR6 and WR7, then a plateau where eight receivers sit inside 11.5 points of each other. Pay for the last seat before the cliff, not for a seat on the plateau.

Ranking players one through forty is a lie we all agree to tell. It implies every step down the list costs the same. It doesn’t. A ranking is a staircase in a house with some very uneven floors, and on Halloween it seems right to mention that one of those steps is a trapdoor.

Here is the shape, from this site’s projections, full PPR over 17 games. Four receivers sit up in the clouds: Puka Nacua at 356.0, Ja’Marr Chase at 336.6, Jaxon Smith‑Njigba at 326.2 and Amon‑Ra St. Brown at 323.3. Then there is a 30.0‑point drop to Justin Jefferson at 293.3 and CeeDee Lamb at 292.7. Then another drop, 23.7 points, to Drake London at 269.0. After that the floor goes flat.

The flat part is the point. From Chris Olave at WR9 (249.7) down to Zay Flowers at WR16 (238.2), eight receivers are separated by 11.5 points across a whole season. That is less than a point and a half per step. Moving from WR9 to WR16 costs you about the same as one bad Sunday from a single player. Moving from WR6 to WR7 costs you 23.7 points, and from WR6 to WR16 costs 54.5.

So the auction logic writes itself. If a bidder pays WR9 money for Olave, he is paying for a spot on a plateau where the next seven players are all within shouting distance. If a bidder pays WR6 money for Lamb, he is buying the last seat before a cliff. The market tends to smear the prices smoothly down the list, as if the staircase were even. The projections say it is not, and that is where the gap lives.

Dallas is the cleanest way to see it, because the Cowboys have a player on each side. Lamb is WR6 at 292.7. George Pickens is WR15 at 238.6. Same offense, same quarterback, 54.1 points apart. The team context is identical, so the entire difference is where each one sits relative to the cliff.

The risk, out loud: a cliff only matters if you can actually reach the top side of it. If Lamb costs Nacua money, the argument is gone, because you are no longer buying the bottom of the cliff, you are buying the sky. This is a bet on price discipline, and projections are a median, not a promise; one receiver injury moves a whole tier. And a plateau is also a bargain bin: if Pickens or Flowers goes for a couple of dollars under the pack, take him gladly.

The Pick: CeeDee Lamb (DAL, WR). Bid up to the price of a WR6 and not a dollar into WR4 territory, because the cliff is only worth it if you land on the right side. If you miss, wait out the plateau and take George Pickens (DAL, WR) or whoever the room lets slide, because a WR16 and a WR9 are 11.5 points apart and should not be 11.5 percent apart on the price tag.

Projected effect: +5% to +10% above his current price
