The Pick

Market Desk·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on your lineup, your waiver claim and your draft board alike.

This week’s boardThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

Theme: scoring settingsRBINDOct 14

Take Away the Point Per Catch and Jonathan Taylor Is the Third‑Best Player in the Pool; Trey McBride Is the Sixty‑Fifth

Jonathan Taylor ranks 8th among running backs, receivers and tight ends in full PPR and 3rd with no credit for catches. Trey McBride goes the other way, from 30th to 65th. Know what your league pays per catch before you bid on either.

Every league has a commissioner who set the scoring in March, forgot about it by April, and has been quietly deciding your auction ever since. One line in the settings, a single point for a catch, moves more money than any sleeper you will find this year. Nobody argues about it, because nobody reads it.

So here is the stress test, built only from the projection set. Take every running back, receiver and tight end, and score him twice: once in full PPR, once with the catch point removed. Ranked against each other, the same pool of players gives two different boards.

The top barely cares. Jahmyr Gibbs is first either way. Puka Nacua is the interesting case: second in PPR with 356.0 points, seventh without the catches at 233.0, because 123 of his points are receptions. Ja’Marr Chase drops from fifth to thirteenth. Justin Jefferson falls from ninth to twenty‑fourth. Receivers are the biggest customers of the catch point, and the pool has them priced as if the point is always there.

Now the other direction. Jonathan Taylor has 315.6 PPR points, eighth in the pool, and only 51 catches among them. Remove the catch point and he has 264.6, which is third, behind only Gibbs and Bijan Robinson. James Cook climbs from thirteenth to sixth on 36 catches. Derrick Henry climbs from fifteenth to fifth on just 21. Meanwhile Trey McBride, the top tight end in the pool at 241.1 points, has 108 catches, or 44.8% of his total. Without them he is at 133.1 and ranks 65th.

The mechanism is that a catch is worth a point whether it goes for two yards or twenty, so PPR pays for volume of targets, not for quality of plays. A receiver who is thrown the ball 140 times is paid for the throwing. A running back who carries it 280 times gets his points from a different tap, one that the catch setting never touches.

Here is the part that matters at an auction. If your room prices off a PPR board and your league pays half a point or nothing per catch, the running backs who earn their points on the ground are on sale, and Taylor is the biggest sale in the pool. His points come from rushing yards and touchdowns, not from the catch line, so every notch you turn the setting down helps him relative to the field.

The risk, out loud: this is a settings argument, not a prediction. In a full PPR league like the site’s own board, Taylor is a fine RB4 and nothing more, and the edge goes away. Taylor’s ground‑heavy line also leans on touches, and the projection set cannot promise 17 healthy games. And we argued yesterday that Henry’s price leans on touchdowns; both can be true. Henry gets here by a different road, a rushing line with almost no catches in it, and he is still the more price‑sensitive of the two.

The line for your league: read the scoring settings before you read the board, because the board was written for somebody else’s.

The Pick: Jonathan Taylor (IND, RB). If your league pays less than a full point a catch, bid him as a top‑three player and not the eighth. If it pays the full point, leave him at his price and let Trey McBride (ARI, TE) be the one you pay for, because his 108 catches are where his value lives.

Projected effect: +8% to +14% above his current price in a league that pays less than a point per catch

Theme: touchdown regressionRBMIAOct 13

Take the Touchdowns Away and De’Von Achane Is the Fourth‑Best Back in the Pool; Derrick Henry Is the Fifteenth

Derrick Henry gets 31.4% of his projected points from touchdowns, the most of any back in the top 14. De’Von Achane gets 16.4%, the least. Pay for the one whose points still show up when the end zone goes quiet.

Touchdowns are the confetti of fantasy football. They are loud, they are fun, and they land wherever the wind puts them. A back can run the same routes, see the same carries, and score on four plays one year and one play the next, and the only thing that changed was a quarterback sneak he had no say in.

So here is a stress test the projection set can run on itself. Take every running back’s projected rushing and receiving touchdowns, multiply by six, and subtract. What is left is the part of the projection that comes from yards and catches: the part that needs a back to be on the field and involved, not lucky. Then re‑rank the position on what is left.

Most of the top ten barely moves. Bijan Robinson climbs to first. Christian McCaffrey and Jahmyr Gibbs swap places and stay in the top three. Jeanty is RB6 either way. Then the confetti shows. James Cook is RB7 at 278.3 points, with 12.6 touchdowns worth 75.6 of them; without those he is at 202.7 and drops to RB12. And Derrick Henry, RB9 at 273.2, has 14.3 touchdowns worth 85.8 points. Without them he is at 187.4, which is RB15.

De’Von Achane is the mirror image. He is RB5 at 292.9, with only 8.0 projected touchdowns worth 48.0 points. Take them all away and he still has 244.9, which ranks fourth. Achane is the one back in the top ten whose price does not need the end zone to cooperate.

The mechanism is that touchdowns are the least repeatable thing a running back does. Yards follow carries and catches, and carries and catches follow a role. Touchdowns follow the red zone, and the red zone follows the schedule, the score and the offensive line’s mood. The projection set has to put a number on them anyway, and for a back who is projected to score a lot, that number is the softest brick in the wall.

That does not make Henry a bad player. He is a good one, and the projection set has him ninth for a reason. It makes him a price‑sensitive one. A room that bids him at RB9 is paying for 14.3 touchdowns arriving on schedule, and if even three of them go missing, that is 18 points, which moves him behind players the room treated as cheaper.

The risk, out loud: Achane’s own floor is not guaranteed. A back who leans on yards and catches needs the volume to hold, and the projection set can’t promise that for 17 games. Also, the touchdown line cuts both ways: an Achane who scores more than 8.0 times is a gift, and a Henry who scores 14 is simply what the room paid for. This is a price argument, not a prediction.

The line for your league: bid on the points that need no luck, and leave the confetti to the other guy.

The Pick: De’Von Achane (MIA, RB). Pay up to his RB5 price and no further, because he is the one back near the top whose price holds up with the touchdowns stripped out. Let the room pay the premium for Derrick Henry (BAL, RB) and his 85.8 touchdown points, and if he falls to you at a discount, treat that as the price of the confetti.

Projected effect: +5% to +9% above his current price

Theme: tier cliffsRBDALOct 12

The Running Back Cliff Is at RB15, and Javonte Williams Is the Last Man Standing on the Ledge

Ten running backs, RB6 through RB15, sit inside 21.0 projected points of each other. Five spots later, the position has fallen off a 49.1‑point cliff. Do not pay RB6 prices for a player the projection set cannot tell from RB15.

Every auction has a guy who treats the sixth running back like a moral victory. He raises his paddle with the calm of a man who has read one article, and he pays for the difference between RB6 and RB11 as if those two players live in different zip codes. They do not. They live in the same apartment and share a Wi‑Fi password.

Here is the shape of the position in the projection set, full PPR over 17 games. The top is real: Jahmyr Gibbs at 364.7, Bijan Robinson at 351.6, Christian McCaffrey at 341.3, then Jonathan Taylor at 315.6 and De’Von Achane at 292.9. From RB3 to RB4 is a 25.7‑point step down, and RB4 to RB5 is another 22.7. Those are stairs you pay for.

Then the stairs stop and the position turns into a landing. Ashton Jeanty is RB6 at 279.6. Nine more backs follow, all the way to Javonte Williams at RB15 with 258.6. That is ten players inside 21.0 points, or 1.2 points a game from the top of the landing to the bottom. No league you will ever play in is decided by 1.2 points a week on a player you could not have picked out of a lineup in the dark.

Now the cliff. Travis Etienne is RB16 at 245.5, which is 13.1 points down in one step. Kyren Williams is RB17 at 231.1, another 14.4. By RB20, D’Andre Swift is at 209.5. From RB15 to RB20 is 49.1 points, more than the entire gap across the ten backs above it. That is the whole column in one sentence: the plateau is wide and flat, and the edge of it is a long way down.

The mechanism is how rooms bid. Bidders rank players, and a ranking feels like a ladder where every rung costs a little more than the one below. The projection set says the middle of this ladder is a floor. If the room pays a premium for each step between RB6 and RB15, it is paying for rungs that are not there, and the money it spends on that is money it does not have for the Gibbs‑to‑Taylor stairs, or for the receiver spots where the gaps are real.

So why Williams and not the cheaper-looking names on the same landing? Because he is the one most likely to be priced like the edge of it. The site’s September 1 insight drop said Jaydon Blue’s exit leaves him with no real competition in Dallas, and the projection set backs that up with 1,266 rushing yards, 11.1 rushing touchdowns and 37 catches. That is a workhorse line, and it lands him inside the landing, not on the cliff. We agree with the earlier call to pay full workhorse price, and we add the reason: the price you should pay is the RB15 price‑plus, because the projection says he is nearly the same player as RB9.

The risk, out loud: a landing is only flat on paper. One injury to the Dallas line, or one coach who decides to give a rookie ten touches, and a flat plateau becomes a hill with Williams at the bottom. Also, the cliff below him is real, which means if you miss on a back in this tier, your replacement is a 13‑point step down, not a shrug. And the market may already have noticed the plateau; the edge here is for rooms that still price RB6 like a destination.

The line for your league: do not pay for the rungs. Pay for the stairs, then wait for the landing.

The Pick: Javonte Williams (DAL, RB). Bid on him as the last seat before the cliff, and let the room spend the extra dollars on Ashton Jeanty (LV, RB) for a difference of 21.0 points that you will not feel in any given week. If you must choose between a back at RB6 and a back at RB15 for the same money, take the one who is still on the landing and keep the change.

Projected effect: +6% to +11% above his current price
