The Pick

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on draft night.

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

Theme: target concentrationWRNOSep 2

Chris Olave Owns a Smaller Slice of New Orleans Than Zay Flowers Owns of Baltimore — and Still Outscores Him by 11.5 Points

Target share tells you who won the job. It does not tell you how big the job is, and New Orleans throws the ball around a lot more than Baltimore does.

Every draft room has a guy who reads one stat and stops reading. He finds a receiver who eats 36% of his team’s targets, calls him a “target hog,” and pays a premium like he just found the building’s sole landlord. Nobody asks how big the building is. Sometimes the landlord owns a shed.

Run Iron Tuna’s 2026 projections and Zay Flowers is that landlord. He is pencilled in for 82 of Baltimore’s 227 projected catches among wideouts and tight ends — a 36.1% share, actually a hair bigger than Justin Jefferson’s 35.6% share of Minnesota’s targets. Same monopoly, roughly. Jefferson finishes the season at 293.3 points, the WR5. Flowers finishes at 238.2, the WR16. A 55‑point gap between two receivers who each own about a third of their team’s passing-game catches.

The math is not complicated once you see it: a share is a fraction, and a fraction is only as good as its denominator. Baltimore’s receiving corps is projected for 227 catches all season, because Derrick Henry and this offense would rather hand it off — the same run‑first identity that made Henry a standard‑league RB5 in this column two weeks ago. Minnesota’s corps catches 312 balls, 85 more, because the Vikings actually throw it. Own a third of a small pie and you still have a small plate.

Chris Olave is the receipt on the other side of the ledger. His 27.7% share looks unremarkable next to Flowers’s 36.1% — a smaller slice, full stop, the number a spreadsheet skimmer discounts him for. But New Orleans’s receiving corps is projected for 328 catches, the most of any team in this table, Puka Nacua’s Rams included. A smaller cut of a bigger number still nets Olave 91 catches and 249.7 points, the WR9 — ahead of Flowers’s WR16 finish despite the “worse” share.

The monopoly is real, too, not just a projection artifact. Our own camp notes from August 28 have Jordyn Tyson’s hamstring injury opening the door for Olave as the unquestioned top target in New Orleans for at least the first month of the season, with Devaughn Vele the only other name drawing snaps behind him. That is exactly the shape of a share worth paying for — a real vacancy, sitting inside a passing offense that already throws more than almost anyone else on this list.

Say the risk out loud, because a column that only says nice things is a press release. Tyson comes back from that hamstring eventually, and every game he plays trims Olave’s slice back down. Flowers is not a bust, either — a Ravens team that falls behind on the scoreboard throws more than this projection assumes, and he already owns the route tree for when that happens. Neither risk changes the number sitting in front of you right now: one of these receivers is priced like a monopoly and is actually running a small shop, and the other is priced like a discount and is actually running the biggest store in the mall.

Do the arithmetic before you pay the “target hog” premium. Multiply the share by the pool it is a share of, not just the percentage on the sheet.

The Pick: Chris Olave (NO, WR) at the discount a 27.7% share earns him on a skim, before the market notices the 328-catch pool it is 27.7% of. Do not pay Flowers-level money for Zay Flowers (BAL, WR) on target share alone — his monopoly is real, but it is a monopoly on a shed.

Projected effect: +10% to +16% above his current WR2-tier price

Theme: committee backfieldsRBPITSep 1

Pittsburgh’s Backfield Is a Photo Finish, and Your League Is Still Betting on One Horse

Jaylen Warren and Rico Dowdle project 5.9 points apart over a full season — a coin flip wearing shoulder pads — and Pittsburgh is not even the closest committee on the board.

Every summer, a beat reporter stands at the practice fence and files some version of the same sentence: both backs are getting first‑team reps, and the staff loves what each of them brings. That sentence gets written so often it should have its own keyboard shortcut. It is not news. It is a coaching staff declining to do a fantasy manager’s job for them, on purpose, for as long as they can get away with it.

Run Iron Tuna’s 2026 projections and the reason for the stalling shows up in the math. Jaylen Warren finishes the season at 191.3 points; Rico Dowdle finishes at 185.4. That is 5.9 points over seventeen games — a third of a point a week, the kind of gap one lucky fumble recovery erases. Warren gets the third‑down work, 46 projected catches to Dowdle’s 29; Dowdle gets the early‑down and goal‑line carries. The model can only project what the depth chart tells it, and right now the depth chart is telling it nothing.

Pittsburgh is not even the tightest race. New England’s Rhamondre Stevenson and TreVeyon Henderson sit 5.2 points apart — 202.0 to 196.8, a gap so small it is really a rounding error wearing a helmet. Two different teams, two different coaching staffs, the same math. When a backfield is this close on paper, it usually means the coaches genuinely have not decided either, and your draft room — which loves a decisive board — is about to invent a decision that does not exist yet.

Here is the part that should worry you before it comforts you: close backfields do not stay close. They resolve, and usually all at once. New Orleans had exactly this kind of committee in July — Alvin Kamara and Travis Etienne within shouting distance of each other on paper — until Kamara sprained his MCL in an August practice and Etienne inherited the job outright. The same kind of math that once had them nearly tied now has them 128.4 points apart. That is what a resolved committee looks like in the numbers, and it is worth remembering every time a five‑point gap feels stable. It is not calm water. It is water that has not decided which way to flow yet.

So do not pay a starter’s price for either half of a true committee, and if you have to pick a side, pick the one who catches the ball. Warren’s 46 projected catches are the reason his 191.3 survives more versions of the season than Dowdle’s 185.4 does — even if Pittsburgh eventually leans on one back for early downs, the receiving role tends to be stickier, because it is the job a passing‑down back keeps even after he loses carries. One town over, the same bet is already paying off: New England’s own camp reports have Henderson’s snap share climbing on Stevenson, which is exactly the shape a resolving committee takes before the box score admits it.

The Pick: Jaylen Warren (PIT, RB) at the discount a true committee back gets, not the discount a healthy scratch gets — his catch role is the safer half of Pittsburgh’s coin flip. If you would rather buy the side that has already started to move, TreVeyon Henderson (NE, RB) is the back whose camp usage is trending up on a price that has not caught up yet.

Projected effect: +9% to +15% above his current committee-back price

Theme: scoring settingsRBBALAug 20

Your league’s scoring setting moves Derrick Henry four spots, and half your room has not opened that page since 2019

The same projections produce a different board depending on what a catch is worth. Nobody prices for it, which is where the money is.

Every fantasy league has one settings page and one guy who has read it. Be that guy. It takes ninety seconds and it is worth more than any sleeper list you will read this month, because the receptions line at the top of that page silently reorders your entire running back board before a single bid is made.

Run our projections at full PPR and Derrick Henry is the RB9, 273.2 points, behind a pile of pass‑catching backs. Run the exact same projections at standard scoring — no point per catch, nothing else changed — and Henry is the RB5 at 252.2, because he only loses the 21 catches he was never getting anyway. Four spots. Same player, same season, same 1,484 rushing yards and thirteen touchdowns. The only thing that moved was a checkbox.

Notice that it cuts both ways, which is what makes it a theme and not a hot take. Jeremiyah Love is our RB8 in full PPR and our RB13 in standard. Ashton Jeanty goes 6 to 12. Those are not small moves, and they are moving in the opposite direction from Henry, which means in a standard league the room is doing the exact wrong thing twice at the same time: paying up for the catches that no longer count, and discounting the 300‑touch back whose entire profile just got more valuable.

The same four hold the top of the board in every format — Jahmyr Gibbs, Bijan Robinson, Christian McCaffrey and Jonathan Taylor take RB1 through RB4 whatever a catch is worth, because they are good at both halves of the job. Elite is elite. It is the RB5‑to‑RB18 band, the part of the board where most of your money actually goes, that shuffles like a deck of cards.

The practical version, since that is the point of all this. In a full PPR league, do not let somebody else’s Henry price talk you out of Love or Jeanty; the catches are real points and you should pay for them. In a half‑PPR or standard league, sit on your hands while the room drafts receptions, and then take the projected top‑five back who is going at RB9 money.

The Pick: Derrick Henry (BAL, RB) in any league that is not full PPR — a top‑five back at his scoring setting, priced by a room that is still reading a PPR ranking list. In full PPR, flip the entry over and buy Jeremiyah Love (ARI, RB) instead.

Projected effect: +15% to +22% above his price in standard and half-PPR scoring
