The Pick

The Pick

Market Desk·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on your lineup, your waiver claim and your draft board alike.

This week’s boardThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

The column
Theme: scoring settingsQBLARNov 13

Two Extra Points per Touchdown Pass Turn Matthew Stafford From QB10 Into QB7. Read Your Settings Before You Read His Price.

Matthew Stafford projects to 288.4 points with four‑point passing touchdowns and 358.2 with six. That moves him past Brock Purdy, Patrick Mahomes and Bo Nix, and to within 5.8 points of Jalen Hurts. Most rooms price him off the four‑point board either way.

Every league has one commissioner who changed the passing touchdown to six points back in 2014 because “it’s worth six on the scoreboard, isn’t it?” Nobody argued, because nobody wanted to be the person who argues about passing touchdowns at a bachelor party. The rule stuck. The cheat sheets did not notice. Most rankings you will find this week still assume four.

That small change is not small for one kind of quarterback. Stafford projects to 34.9 touchdown passes, the most in the pool, and 39 rushing yards, which is roughly one scramble to avoid a sack and one kneel‑down he forgot to take. With four‑point touchdowns, 48% of his points come from throwing scores. For Josh Allen it is 29%. For Hurts it is 30%. For Jayden Daniels it is 29%. Those three get paid for running, and running pays the same in every league. Stafford gets paid for touchdown passes, and those are exactly what the commissioner repriced.

So add two points per touchdown pass and watch the board move. Stafford gains 69.8 points, the most of any quarterback, and climbs from QB10 to QB7. Joe Burrow, the other pure thrower, gains 66.0 and jumps from QB6 to QB4. Hurts gains 47.0 and slips to QB5. Purdy, who was 0.7 points ahead of Stafford, is now 14.9 behind him. Daniels was 25.6 points ahead and is now 0.6 ahead, which is less than one bad interception.

Where this sits with the rest of the site: our August 27 insight drop said Stafford is expensive for a quarterback who does not run, but that the case is real. Both halves are right. Your scoring page decides which half applies to you. In a four‑point league he is QB10 in a crowd, and our November 9 entry already told you quarterbacks in that crowd are cheap to shop for. In a six‑point league he leaves the crowd. He is the cheaper version of Burrow, and he sits less than a point behind Daniels.

The risk, out loud: a projection built on touchdown passes is a projection built on the most random stat a quarterback has. If Stafford throws 28 instead of 34.9, the six‑point bonus shrinks by about 14 points and he lands back near Purdy. He also gives you no rushing floor on the weeks the touchdowns go to the running backs. You are buying a bigger ceiling, not a safer player.

The Pick: Matthew Stafford (LAR, QB). In a six‑point passing touchdown league, bid him as a top‑seven quarterback and let someone else pay the rushing premium for Jalen Hurts (PHI, QB), who is only 5.8 points better there. In a four‑point league, leave Stafford at his QB10 price and do not chase.

Projected effect: +10% to +18% above his current price in six‑point passing touchdown leagues

Theme: rookie pricingWRCLENov 12

Price the Rookie by His Job, Not His Draft Slot. Denzel Boston, a Second‑Rounder, Projects 88.5 Points Above the Eighth Overall Pick.

Denzel Boston projects to 141.2 points, WR58 and ahead of his own team’s veteran Jerry Jeudy. Jordyn Tyson, taken eighth overall, projects to 52.7. The market still prices rookies by where they were drafted.

Every April, a rookie walks across a stage, puts on a hat, and hugs a man in a suit. Fantasy players watch that hug and write a number next to his name. The number is the draft slot. It is a fine measure of what a front office thought about a college player. It is a terrible measure of how many passes he will catch in your league this year, because the hat does not come with a job.

The projection set makes the point with three receivers. Jordyn Tyson went eighth overall to New Orleans. He opened the year on injured reserve with a hamstring, and he projects to 52.7 points, WR104. Omar Cooper Jr. went 30th to the Jets and opened camp behind Adonai Mitchell as the third option: 86.7 points, WR85. Denzel Boston went in the second round to Cleveland and won an outside job in camp: 141.2 points, WR58. The latest pick of the three projects for the most points, by 54.5 over Cooper and 88.5 over Tyson.

The mechanism is boring, which is why it works. Rookie points come from snaps, and snaps come from the depth chart, not the draft board. When Cleveland released Cedric Tillman at cutdown day, the two outside jobs next to Jeudy went to KC Concepcion and Boston. The projection gives Boston 55 catches for 694 yards. It gives Jeudy, the veteran who used to be the answer, 52 catches for 722. Boston finishes 1.8 points ahead of him. Concepcion, the first‑rounder, leads both at 154.8, and he is only 13.6 points clear of Boston. A full round of draft capital buys less than a point a week.

The rule runs the other way, too. Jeremiyah Love went third overall and projects as RB8 with 276.8 points. That is not a rookie premium. That is a job, and the price should follow it. Jadarian Price, Seattle’s first‑round back, projects as RB25 with 193.6 because Seattle handed him the early work. Pay for the job in both directions: full price for a rookie who has one, bench money for a rookie who does not, no matter what the hat said.

Where this sits with the rest of the site: our camp desk said on August 30 that Boston was worth a small late‑round dollar and not starter money. We agree on the second half and go one step further on the first. The projection now has him ahead of Jeudy, so he is not a lottery ticket. He is a fifth receiver with a starting job, and he should be priced like one.

The risk, out loud: Cleveland’s quarterbacks project to about 3,700 passing yards combined, and Harold Fannin Jr. soaks up 84 catches at tight end. That is a small pie with a big eater at the table. Boston is also worth less without the point per catch, where he drops to 86.2. He is a depth piece with a real role, not a breakout you build around.

The Pick: Denzel Boston (CLE, WR). Bid him like the WR5 he projects to be, at least as much as you would pay for Jeudy, and stop bidding before starter money. Skip Jordyn Tyson (NO, WR) at anything above a bench dollar until he is back on the field and the job is his.

Projected effect: +12% to +20% above his current price

Theme: committee backfieldsRBPITNov 11

A Committee Is One Job With Two Price Tags. Rico Dowdle Is the Cheap One.

Jaylen Warren projects to 191.3 points and Rico Dowdle to 185.4, a gap of 5.9 over a full season. In standard scoring Dowdle is the better back by 11.1. The market still prices them as a starter and a backup.

Every committee backfield comes with a name tag. One back is “the starter,” the other is “the other guy,” and your league pays for the name tag as if it came with a raise. Usually it doesn’t. Usually it is a sticker the beat writers put on one of two men who are about to split the same pile of carries.

Pittsburgh is the cleanest case on the board. The Steelers have two pure running backs on the roster and nobody else, and the projections split the work almost down the middle. Dowdle gets the early downs: 975 rushing yards and 5.5 rushing touchdowns. Warren gets the passing downs: 46 catches to Dowdle’s 29. Add it up in full PPR and Warren wins by 5.9 points. That is 0.35 points a week, or roughly the value of one checkdown every three Sundays.

Now flip the scoring. Take the point per catch away and Dowdle jumps from RB30 to RB27 while Warren drops from RB26 to RB31. The “backup” is the better standard-league back. In half PPR they are close to a coin flip. There is no scoring setting in which the gap between these two is worth a starter’s premium.

It is not just Pittsburgh. Look at the four most even backfields in the pool. In New England, Rhamondre Stevenson leads TreVeyon Henderson by 5.2. In Washington, Rachaad White leads Jacory Croskey-Merritt by 5.4. In Carolina, Chuba Hubbard leads Jonathon Brooks by 1.6, which is less than one long run. The mechanism is the same everywhere: a committee is one starting job cut in two, and the projection splits the points the way the coaches split the snaps. The market splits the price the way the depth chart lists the names.

Why Dowdle and not the other three? Because the site has already argued the others. The Play-Caller column called Henderson the riser in New England and Croskey-Merritt the lead back in Washington, and the Carolina picture has moved around all summer. Pittsburgh is the one where the roster is settled and the price still isn’t. Our auction desk said in September to pay Warren like a clear lead back once the Steelers cut to two. We read the same roster and land a step to the side: two backs and nobody else means the split is the whole story, and the projection says the split is nearly even. Pay Warren fairly. Just do not pay him a lead back’s premium over Dowdle.

The risk, out loud: Mike McCarthy has a long habit of sharing the ball, and that cuts both ways. If he leans on Warren’s hands in a trailing game script, Dowdle’s weeks get thin, and his touchdowns depend on Pittsburgh reaching the goal line, which is never promised. He is a flex play with a starter’s workload, not a star.

The Pick: Rico Dowdle (PIT, RB). Buy the cheap half of the committee. If your league prices him as Warren’s backup, pay up to Warren’s price minus a few dollars and enjoy the same job for less. In a standard league, take Dowdle first.

Projected effect: +8% to +13% above his current price
