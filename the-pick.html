The Pick

The Pick

Market Desk·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on your lineup, your waiver claim and your draft board alike.

This week’s boardThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

Theme: touchdown regressionTECLENov 26

Touchdowns Are the Wishbone and Catches Are the Turkey. Harold Fannin Jr. Brought the Turkey, and Mark Andrews Brought a Wishbone.

The top 24 tight ends project to one receiving touchdown for every 15.4 catches. Give every one of them that same rate and Harold Fannin Jr. gains 13.6 points and moves from TE5 to TE4, while Mark Andrews loses 20.0 and drops from TE12 to TE17.

It is Thanksgiving, which means that somewhere today two grown adults will each grab one end of a turkey bone, make a wish and pull. One of them will win. Neither of them will have earned it. Nobody at the table says, “Uncle Rick won the wishbone, so let’s give him the big chair next year.” Everyone knows the wishbone is luck. Remember the wishbone. It comes back.

Touchdowns are the wishbone of fantasy football. Catches and yards are the turkey. The turkey shows up every week. The wishbone shows up when a play near the goal line happens to go your way. The site’s projections give the top 24 tight ends 110.4 receiving touchdowns on 1,698 catches, a rate of 6.5 touchdowns per 100 catches. Most of the group sits close to that. Two names sit far from it. Fannin projects to 84 catches and only 3.2 receiving touchdowns, 3.8 per 100. Andrews projects to 58 catches and 7.1 receiving touchdowns, 12.2 per 100. That is nearly double the group rate, on the fewest catches of anyone in the top 12.

Read the table as two piles. In the top pile are the tight ends who catch a lot of balls and get short‑changed on touchdowns: Fannin, Kyle Pitts and T.J. Hockenson each project under 4.1 touchdowns per 100 catches, and each gains 12 to 14 points at the group rate. In the bottom pile are the ones whose totals lean on the end zone. Andrews gets 42.6 of his 169.1 points from receiving touchdowns, 25.2%, the biggest share of any top‑24 tight end. Take the touchdowns out of everyone and Fannin ranks fifth at 167.5 points. Andrews ranks 18th at 122.3.

The mechanism is the reason this is a buy and not a coin flip. Catches come from being the player the quarterback looks for on every down, and that carries from week to week and year to year. Touchdowns come from a handful of snaps inside the 10, and those bounce. A tight end with 84 catches will get his share of those snaps just by being on the field for them. A tight end with 58 catches needs every one of his to turn into a score. If both drift toward the middle, and the middle is where touchdown rates go, the gap between them grows from 19.4 points to 53.0.

Where this sits with the rest of the site. The Play‑Caller Premium’s August 19 call said Todd Monken feeds the tight end everywhere he goes, and named Fannin as the man who gets that job in Cleveland. Our August 20 insight drop said to buy Fannin as a second‑wave tight end. We agree with both, and this table adds one more reason: the projection already gives him the catches and has not yet given him the touchdowns that usually come with them. On Andrews, our August 27 insight drop called him touchdown‑reliant and weak in full PPR, and the Play‑Caller’s September 4 call noted that the coach who built his role is now in Cleveland. We agree with both of those too. Monken took the turkey with him. Baltimore kept the wishbone.

The risk, out loud: 6.5 per 100 is an average, not a law. Some tight ends score above it for years, and Andrews has been one of them; he scored 11 times on 55 catches in 2024. A low touchdown rate can also be real if the offense around a player struggles to reach the goal line, and our August 20 drop rated Fannin’s call only medium confidence because a new scheme can land slowly. Even so, his floor is the 84 catches, and those do not need luck. Back to the table. Nobody hands Uncle Rick the big chair for winning the wishbone. Do not hand Andrews a top‑12 price for winning it either.

The Pick: Harold Fannin Jr. (CLE, TE). Bid him as a top‑5 tight end, at or above Loveland’s price, and treat the touchdowns he is owed as your profit. On the other side, price Mark Andrews (BAL, TE) as the TE17 he is without the extra scores, not the TE12 he is with them, and let someone else pull the wishbone.

Projected effect: +10% to +16% above his current price

Theme: scoring settingsQBLARNov 25

In a Six‑Point Passing League, Matthew Stafford Is Jayden Daniels Without the Legs. Check Your Settings Before You Pay Either One.

Move a passing touchdown from four points to six and Matthew Stafford gains 69.8 points, more than any quarterback in the pool, and climbs from QB10 to QB7. His edge over the 12th quarterback goes from 4.5 points to 22.8, almost exactly Jayden Daniels’s 23.4.

Every family trip abroad has one person who brought only cash. At home, cash is cash. Then the plane lands, the exchange rate moves, and the person with a fat wallet of one currency is suddenly richer or poorer than everyone else at the table, without doing a single thing. Remember the person with the wallet. They come back.

Your league’s scoring page is an exchange rate. Most leagues pay four points for a passing touchdown, and every number on this site’s boards uses four. Plenty of leagues pay six. That one line does not move every quarterback the same amount, because quarterbacks do not carry the same wallet. A runner like Daniels gets 97.6 of his 314.0 projected points on the ground, 31.1% of his total, and rushing points do not change when the passing rate does. Stafford projects to 39 rushing yards and no rushing touchdowns. That is 3.9 points, 1.4% of his total. Everything he has is in passing touchdowns, and he projects to 34.9 of them, the most in the pool.

Read the last column, because that is the one your money follows. A quarterback is worth what he beats the last starter by, not his raw total. With four‑point touchdowns, Stafford beats Prescott by 4.5 points, a quarter of a point a game. He is a body. With six, he beats Lawrence by 22.8, and Daniels beats him by 23.4. Same players, same projections, and the gap between them goes from 25.6 points to 0.6. Burrow takes the same trip for the same reason: 33.0 passing touchdowns and only 31.5 rushing points carry him from QB6 to QB4, ahead of Hurts and Daniels.

The mechanism is simple enough to say at a bar. Six‑point scoring is a tax cut for passers and nothing at all for runners. Every quarterback gets richer in raw points, so raw points are the wrong thing to look at. What changes is the order, and the order is what you pay for. The runners slide a spot or two. The pure throwers with big touchdown counts jump over the middle of the board, which is a crowd of Nix, Purdy and Mahomes separated by 3.6 points under the site’s scoring.

Where this sits with the rest of the site. Our August 27 insight drop said Stafford is expensive for a quarterback who does not run, but that the case is real, because Puka Nacua and Davante Adams give him two proven targets near the goal line. We agree, and the sheet backs it: those two project to 20.3 receiving touchdowns between them. Our only addition is that how real the case is depends on one line in your league settings. With four‑point touchdowns, the August 27 “expensive” is the right word. With six, he is priced like the QB10 he is not.

The risk, out loud: touchdowns are the stat that wanders most, and Stafford’s whole profile is touchdowns. If he throws 28 instead of 34.9, the six‑point bonus shrinks with them, and he has no rushing floor to land on. He also turns 39 in February, and a passer who cannot move is one bad week of blocking from a bad week of points. Daniels’s legs are a safer floor in any scoring. Back to the person with the wallet. When the exchange rate moves in their favor, they did not get smarter. They just showed up holding the right money. Make sure you know which country your league is in before you count anyone’s cash.

The Pick: Matthew Stafford (LAR, QB). In a league that pays six points for a passing touchdown, bid him like Jayden Daniels, not like QB10, because his edge over a replacement starter is the same. In a four‑point league, leave him at his QB10 price and let someone else pay for the touchdowns. If you miss him in a six‑point league, Joe Burrow (CIN, QB) is the same trade one tier higher.

Projected effect: +12% to +20% above his current price in six‑point passing leagues

Theme: the cost of an injury handcuffRBDETNov 24

Most Handcuffs Are a Spare‑Tire Well With No Tire in It. Isiah Pacheco Is a Full‑Size Spare Bolted to the Best Car on the Lot.

Isiah Pacheco owns 26.3% of Detroit’s projected running‑back rushing yards, the biggest share of any backup in the table below, and the starter he backs up, Jahmyr Gibbs, is the RB1. A handcuff is insurance, and his is the biggest policy that also pays while nothing goes wrong.

Open the trunk of a used car and you find one of three things. A full‑size spare, which gets you home. A donut, which gets you to a shop at 50 miles an hour while everyone passes you. Or the little foam cutout shaped like a tire, with no tire in it, which gets you a long talk with a tow truck driver. All three cost the same at the dealership, because nobody opens the trunk. Remember the foam cutout. It comes back.

A handcuff is a policy on your best running back, so judge it like a policy: how big is the claim, and what do you get while you wait for one? On the first, nobody beats Detroit. The site’s projections make Gibbs the RB1 at 364.7 points, 21.5 a game. That is the biggest weekly hole in the running‑back pool if he misses time. On the second, look at what the backup already does with the starter healthy. Pacheco projects to 489 rushing yards and 3.4 rushing touchdowns, which is 69.3 points from running alone and 26.3% of the 1,862 rushing yards Detroit’s backs project to. He is not a note on the depth chart. He has a job.

Why rushing share and not total points? Because a policy pays out in the job you lose, not the job you keep. Justice Hill projects to more points than Pacheco, 114.0 to 93.8, but 39 catches make up most of that. He gets 223 rushing yards to Derrick Henry’s 1,484. If Henry goes down, the sheet has Hill as the passing‑down back, not the man already taking early‑down carries. That is a donut. It works, but it is not the tire you had. Pacheco is already doing the starter’s kind of work, just less of it, so when he gets more of it nobody has to guess who gets the ball.

And then there is Indianapolis. Jonathan Taylor is the RB4 at 315.6 points, and the best backup in the pool behind him is Seth McGowan at 15.8, with 52 rushing yards. That is the foam cutout. The projection is not saying McGowan inherits 1,500 yards if Taylor gets hurt. It is saying nobody knows who does, and a handcuff you cannot name is just a bench spot you paid for.

Where this sits with the rest of the site. Our August 27 camp report said to keep Pacheco priced as a real handcuff to Gibbs and not discount him for his camp MCL sprain. The Play‑Caller Premium’s August 22 call went further and said Dan Campbell’s fourth‑down habit makes Pacheco a touchdown source even with Gibbs healthy. We agree with both. Our August 18 camp report called him a one‑to‑two dollar handcuff, not a standalone play, and the sheet agrees he is not a weekly starter at RB51. Our only quarrel is the price tag. The best policy in the table should not cost the same as the foam cutout.

The risk, out loud: insurance only pays if something happens, and a healthy Gibbs leaves Pacheco at about 5.5 points a game, which will not crack your lineup. Pacheco’s 3.4 rushing touchdowns are 19.0% of Detroit’s backfield total, and touchdowns wander. And a handcuff is worth the most to the person who owns the starter. To everyone else he is a lottery ticket, so price him by whose roster he is going to. Back to the trunk. With the fantasy playoffs close, the Gibbs manager is the one driver who should check the spare before the trip, because his car is the one everyone else would steal the tire from.

The Pick: Isiah Pacheco (DET, RB). If you roster Gibbs, he is the one handcuff in the league worth a real bid or a real waiver claim, not the minimum. If you do not, grab him anyway as the biggest payout on the board if Detroit’s starter misses a week, and skip the backups in Indianapolis entirely.

Projected effect: +10% to +16% above a handcuff-only price
