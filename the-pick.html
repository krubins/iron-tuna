The Pick

The Pick

Market Desk·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on your lineup, your waiver claim and your draft board alike.

This week’s boardThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

The column
Theme: tier cliffsWRMINNov 17

The Big Cliff at Receiver Is Half Touchdowns. The Real One Sits Under CeeDee Lamb, and Justin Jefferson Is Standing on the Right Side of It.

Amon‑Ra St. Brown projects 30.0 points ahead of Justin Jefferson, the biggest step in the top ten. Score both at the same touchdown rate and it shrinks to 14.0. The drop from Lamb at WR6 to Drake London at WR7 is 23.7 points, and it barely moves: 20.6 after the same check.

Ski resorts paint a black diamond at the top of a trail when it gets steep. Sometimes the trail really is steep all the way down. Sometimes it is a blue run with one icy patch near the top, and the sign is about the ice. You only find out which by skiing it. Fantasy boards work the same way. A cliff on a cheat sheet tells the room where to stop paying, and nobody checks whether it is rock or ice.

Here is the cheat sheet at receiver. Puka Nacua sits alone at 356.0. Then Ja’Marr Chase, Jaxon Smith‑Njigba and St. Brown, all within 13.3 points of each other. Then the big step: 30.0 points down to Jefferson at 293.3, with Lamb 0.6 behind him. Then another step, 23.7 points down to London. For scale, the whole distance from WR9 Chris Olave to WR18 Davante Adams is 17.3 points. On paper the board has two cliffs, and the first one is the bigger.

Now check the rock. Two days ago this column measured a house rate for the top 36 receivers: 0.63 touchdowns per 100 scrimmage yards. Score every receiver in the top eight at that rate and look again. St. Brown projects to 10.5 touchdowns on 1,439 yards, where the house rate gives 9.1. Jefferson projects to 7.5 on 1,387, where the house rate gives 8.7. Even them out and St. Brown is 314.7 and Jefferson is 300.7. The 30.0‑point cliff is now 14.0, less than a point a week. Lamb to London goes from 23.7 to 20.6. That one is rock.

Why does one cliff melt and the other hold? Look at what is left when you take touchdowns out completely. St. Brown’s catches and yards are worth 260.3 points, Jefferson’s 248.3: a 12.0‑point gap. Lamb’s are worth 240.5 and London’s 225.2: a 15.3‑point gap. London catches one fewer ball than Lamb, but he gains 126 fewer yards doing it. Catches and yards come from targets and role, and they repeat. Touchdowns come from the red zone, and they wander. The step under Lamb is built from the part that repeats. The step under St. Brown is mostly the part that wanders.

The digression pays off here. Jefferson is the receiver who led the whole league with 128 catches and 1,809 yards in 2022. He has always been a yards player first, and a yards player is exactly the receiver a touchdown cliff shortchanges: the board ranks him on the part that wanders and ignores the part that repeats.

Where this sits with the rest of the site: our July 30 insight drop said not to fade St. Brown on “too expensive” schedule arguments, and we are not fading him. He is a top‑four receiver in both columns of the table. The claim is narrower: the gap between him and Jefferson is about half what the cheat sheet says, so the price gap should be too.

The risk, out loud: not every touchdown gap is luck. Jared Goff projects to 29.0 touchdown passes. Kyler Murray, Minnesota’s quarterback in our pool, projects to 19.5. Jefferson already catches more than a third of those, so the house rate may be asking a weaker passing game for scores it cannot throw. Call the true gap somewhere between 14.0 and 30.0. Even at the top of that range, Jefferson is on the high side of the cliff that holds.

The Pick: Justin Jefferson (MIN, WR). Treat him as the last receiver above the real cliff, not the first one below a fake one, and bid him to within a few dollars of St. Brown. Do not let Drake London (ATL, WR) go for Jefferson or Lamb money; he is the first receiver under the drop that survives the check.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% above his current price

Theme: the shape of the moneyRBKCNov 16

Eight Running Backs Sit Inside Nine Points. Pay for the Last One on the Shelf, and Kenneth Walker III Is the Last One Worth Buying.

From RB6 Ashton Jeanty (279.6) to RB13 Chase Brown (270.7), eight running backs project within 8.9 points of each other. Kenneth Walker III is RB11 at 272.6, 7.0 points behind Jeanty, which is less than half a point a week. The room will price the eight by rank anyway.

Walk into any grocery store and look at the cereal. The box at eye level costs a dollar more than the box on the bottom shelf, and the only difference is that you have to bend your knees to reach the cheap one. Stores know shoppers pay for where a thing sits, not what is in it. Auction rooms work the same way, and the eye‑level box at running back is called “RB6.”

Here is the shape of the money at the position. On top sit the stars: Jahmyr Gibbs at 364.7, Bijan Robinson at 351.6 and Christian McCaffrey at 341.3. Gibbs alone is 85.1 points clear of RB6, about five points a week, and that gap is worth paying for. Then the board drops through Jonathan Taylor and De’Von Achane and lands on a flat shelf. Jeanty, James Cook, Jeremiyah Love, Derrick Henry, Saquon Barkley, Walker, Breece Hall and Brown: eight backs, 8.9 points from first to last. After Brown it falls again, 9.0 points to Omarion Hampton and 34.1 below Jeanty by the time you reach Travis Etienne at RB16.

Set replacement at the 30th back, Rico Dowdle, at 185.4, the same line this column used for running backs before. Jeanty is 94.2 points above it. Walker is 87.2. So the RB11 gives you 93% of the RB6’s edge, and in most rooms he costs noticeably less, because bidders pay for the number next to the name. Buy two backs from the front of the shelf, Jeanty and Cook, and you get 557.9 points. Buy two from the back, Walker and Hall, and you get 543.9. That is 14.0 points, under a point a week, for what is usually a real chunk of budget. Spend that chunk on a receiver or a better quarterback instead.

Why Walker and not just any name at the back of the shelf? Because his points rest on a job, not a guess. He projects to 1,239 rushing yards, 9.1 rushing touchdowns and 48 catches, and no other Chiefs back in the pool projects to even 65 points. Our June 21 camp report said he had a clear path in Kansas City and called him a mid‑range RB1 worth paying for. The projection agrees with the role. It just ranks him eleventh, and that ranking is your discount. He also projects to the fewest lost fumbles on the shelf, 0.9, which is not a reason to buy anyone but is a nice thing to say at the draft.

Where this sits with the rest of the site: our June 26 camp report said to pay up for Jeanty as a workhorse, and we are not walking that back. He is still the top of the shelf. We are saying the top of the shelf is only 7.0 points above Walker, and Jeanty’s August knee scare, which turned into an ankle sprain that kept him uncleared for Week 1 in our September 2 report, is the kind of thing a 7‑point edge does not cover. Pay for Jeanty like the shelf he is on, not like the label.

The risk, out loud: a flat shelf is flat only on paper. The eight backs are not the same player, and one of them will finish in the top five. The stars are a different decision entirely, and nothing here says to skip Gibbs if you can afford him. The point is narrower: once you are shopping the shelf, the projection cannot tell the boxes apart, so the price should be the thing you pick on.

The Pick: Kenneth Walker III (KC, RB). Treat him as a full member of the RB6 to RB13 shelf and bid him up to what the room pays for the front of it. Do not pay a dollar more for Ashton Jeanty (LV, RB) than you would for Walker. Bend your knees.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% above his current price

Theme: touchdown regressionWRNYJNov 15

Davante Adams Is 25 Points of Touchdown Luck. Garrett Wilson Is 13 Points of Bad Luck. Buy the Bad Luck.

Davante Adams projects to 10.6 touchdowns on 1,016 yards. At the rate the top 36 receivers score, he would have 6.4 and fall from WR18 to the mid‑20s. Garrett Wilson projects to 5.3 touchdowns on 1,183 yards. At that same rate he would have 7.5 and climb from WR12, where he sits in a tie with Nico Collins, to WR8.

Every league has a guy who checks his phone after the early games and says, “My receiver had four catches and I won the week.” He is not wrong. He is just describing a coin that landed on its edge. Touchdowns are the lottery ticket stapled to every stat line, and the trick at the auction is to pay for the paycheck, not the ticket.

Here is the check. Take the 36 best receivers in this site’s projections. Together they cover 40,606 scrimmage yards and score 255.8 touchdowns, a rate of 0.63 per 100 yards. Call that the house rate. Most of the room sits within a touchdown of it. Adams does not. He scores 10.6 times on 1,016 yards, one touchdown every 6.4 catches. At the house rate that is 6.4 touchdowns, 25.2 points gone, and the biggest gap of any receiver in the top 36. Wilson runs the other way: 100 catches, 5.3 touchdowns, one every 19 catches. At the house rate he gains 12.9 points and passes everyone but the top seven.

The mechanism is simple. Yards follow targets, and targets follow talent and role, which carry over from year to year. Touchdowns follow field position, play calls and who happens to be open on third‑and‑goal, which do not. The Rams give Adams a great setup for it: Matthew Stafford projects to 34.9 touchdown passes, and Adams catches 30% of them while catching far fewer balls than Puka Nacua. The Jets are the opposite. Geno Smith projects to 20.4, and Wilson, who catches more passes than any two other Jets combined, gets about a quarter of them.

Now the digression. In 2020 Adams caught 18 touchdowns in 14 games for Green Bay, which is the kind of season people still bring up at bars. It is also the reason this entry exists. A receiver with a famous touchdown year gets priced like the famous year is his normal year forever. It was not his normal year then, and at 33, on a team where Nacua is the first read, it is not going to become his normal year now. You are paying for a highlight reel, and the highlight reel does not come with a refund.

Where this sits with the rest of the site: our July 3 and July 21 camp reports both called Wilson a value, pointing at the targets he earned in his healthy seasons and a market that had him going in the WR15 to WR19 range. We agree, and this is the second reason. The market prices the Jets’ mess. It should also price the fact that the mess has cost Wilson touchdowns he was owed. Yards are the part of his game that does not depend on the mess, and he has plenty of them.

The risk, out loud: the house rate is an average, and the Rams really are a better place to score than the Jets. Stafford throws 14.5 more touchdown passes than Smith in our numbers, so some of Adams’s edge is real and some of Wilson’s gap is earned. Wilson is also coming back from the knee injury that ended his last season early. None of that changes the direction. It changes how hard you lean.

The Pick: Garrett Wilson (NYJ, WR). Bid him as a top‑12 receiver, not the WR15 the room wants, and let the touchdowns he is owed be your profit. On the other side, price Davante Adams (LAR, WR) as the low‑end WR2 his yards say he is, and let someone else pay for 2020.

Projected effect: +6% to +11% above his current price
