The Pick

Nate Vega·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on your lineup, your waiver claim and your draft board alike.

This week’s boardThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

Running Back Has a Plateau, Not a Ladder, and Javonte Williams Is the Cheap Seat on It

Nine running backs, RB7 through RB15, sit inside 19.7 PPR points of each other. Past that plateau the board drops 13.1 points to the next back. Pay for the cliff edge, not for the rung you happen to like.

An auction room treats a ranking like a ladder: every rung is a little better than the one below it, so every rung gets a little more money. That is a lovely picture and it is not how running backs are shaped. The board is more like a staircase built by someone who got bored. A few big steps, a long flat landing, then a hole.

Start at the top. Jahmyr Gibbs (DET) projects for 364.7 PPR points, Bijan Robinson (ATL) for 351.6 and Christian McCaffrey (SF) for 341.3. Jonathan Taylor (IND) is next at 315.6, a 25.7‑point drop from McCaffrey, and Achane (MIA) is 22.7 behind Taylor at 292.9. Those are real steps. If you want to pay for a cliff, pay for the one between the first four and everybody else.

Now the landing. James Cook (BUF) is RB7 at 278.3. Jeremiyah Love, Derrick Henry, Saquon Barkley, Kenneth Walker III, Breece Hall, Chase Brown and Omarion Hampton follow, and the ninth back on the landing is Javonte Williams (DAL) at 258.6. That is the whole plateau: 19.7 points across nine players, about 1.2 points a game from the top of it to the bottom. Walker and Barkley are tied at 272.6. No room should be putting a multi‑dollar gap between players a rounding error apart.

And then the hole. The next back, Travis Etienne (NO), is at 245.5, which is 13.1 points under Williams. After him it is Kyren Williams (LAR) at 231.1, then Cam Skattebo and Quinshon Judkins at 226.5 and 226.4. So the plateau is the last neighborhood where a back costs you no real production. Fall off the edge and you give up 13 points the instant you go one name down.

So why Williams, the last name on the landing? Because he is the one the room is least likely to price like a member of it. He is the bottom of the group on points, so he draws the smallest bid, and the cost of being wrong about the order is 1.2 points a week. And the role is not in doubt. The September 1 camp report says Jaydon Blue’s exit leaves him with no real competition in Dallas, and the site’s call that day was to pay full workhorse price for him. This column does not argue with it. It argues that “full workhorse price” should be the bottom of the plateau’s price, not the top.

The risk, out loud: his line leans on the end zone. He is projected for 11.1 rushing touchdowns, which is 66.6 of his 258.6 points, against only 37 catches and 218 receiving yards. Touchdowns are the part of a season that comes and goes. A back like that can finish 20 points under the projection and nobody will be surprised, and if he does he falls into the hole with Etienne. Receiving backs like Love, with 65 catches, and Hall, with 52, have a sturdier floor for the same money.

The line to take to your league: running back is not a ladder, it is a landing, and the only rung worth a premium is the last one before the hole.

The Pick: Javonte Williams (DAL, RB), and bid him as the cheapest back on the plateau, never above where the room puts Barkley or Walker. If somebody bids past Cook’s number on any of the nine, let them; you will get the same points for less from the last chair. Do not chase a back off the edge for the sake of a name.

Projected effect: +6% to +11% above his current price

Theme: positional scarcityTELVOct 6

There Are Two Tight Ends and Then There Is a Waiting Room, and Brock Bowers Is the Twin You Can Buy Without the Name Tax

Trey McBride projects for 241.1 PPR points and Brock Bowers for 240.4. The next tight end is 30.4 points back, so the position is two real assets and a crowd. Take whichever twin the room charges less for.

Scarcity is the most overused word in an auction room. Somebody says “tight end is thin” and three people reach for their wallets, as if thin were a price instead of a shape. Thin only matters if you can measure how thin, so let’s measure it. Count how many points a player gives you over the free replacement you could pick up for nothing, and see who is actually scarce.

Set the replacement bar at the last starter in a 12‑team league: QB12, RB24, WR36, TE12. That bar is 285.6 points at quarterback, 196.8 at running back, 184.4 at receiver and 169.1 at tight end. McBride clears his bar by 72.0 points and Bowers by 71.3. That sounds like a lot until you line it up against the whole pool: McBride is the 23rd most valuable player by that measure, Bowers the 24th. Puka Nacua clears his bar by 171.6. Scarcity at tight end is real, but it is a small number.

Here is the part the room misses. The cliff after the pair is steep. Tyler Warren (IND) is the TE3 at 210.0, which is 30.4 points behind Bowers, and he is only the 39th most valuable player at 40.9 points over replacement. So you are not buying tight end value in general. You are buying exactly two seats at a table, and a third and fourth chair that are already worth about half as much.

Why does that matter for the twins? Because the two are 0.7 points apart over a season, about four hundredths of a point a week, and the room will not price them that way. One of them gets the headline and the dollars that come with it. McBride is the one the site has already backed: the August 30 Play-Caller column says his role is the part of Arizona’s offense a bad quarterback cannot shrink, and nothing here disagrees. He catches more, 108 to 99. Bowers scores more, 6.6 receiving touchdowns to 5.3. Different roads, same address.

So this is not an argument against McBride. It is an argument against paying a tax for him. If the room puts McBride a few dollars above Bowers, the extra money buys you 0.7 points, and you should hand that premium to somebody else. If the room runs it the other way, and Bowers is the one wearing the tax, buy McBride and keep the receipt for the Play-Caller call.

The alternative is the waiting room. Past Warren the board flattens fast: Harold Fannin Jr. (188.5), Sam LaPorta (188.3), George Kittle (187.8) and Kyle Pitts (184.4) sit inside four points of each other. Andrews at TE12 is projected for 7.1 receiving touchdowns on only 58 catches, which is the kind of line that comes back toward the middle. Paying for that is paying for a tail.

The risk, out loud: this is a model, and a tight end who touches the ball 99 times is exposed to one bad week of targets or one bad ankle. Las Vegas could also bury him in a game script he cannot catch his way out of. And if your room already prices the pair within a dollar, there is no tax to dodge. Check the board first.

The line to take to your league: scarcity is not a position, it is a gap. At tight end the gap is 30.4 points wide and exactly two players stand on the good side of it.

The Pick: Brock Bowers (LV, TE), and bid him within a dollar of what Trey McBride (ARI, TE) costs. If McBride is the one carrying the premium, take Bowers; if Bowers is, take McBride. Either way, one of the two, and nobody from the waiting room.

Projected effect: +5% to +9% above his current price

Theme: committee backfieldsRBPITOct 5

Pittsburgh’s Backfield Is a Coin Flip With Two Heads, and Rico Dowdle Is the Side Nobody Is Pricing

Jaylen Warren projects for 191.3 PPR points and Rico Dowdle for 185.4, a gap of 5.9 over 17 games. Together they are 376.7, more than any single back in this pool. Pay them as a pair, not as a lead back and a spare.

A committee backfield is a group project. Everybody agrees it will be done, nobody agrees who is doing it, and the one person who handed in a clean page gets the credit. In auction rooms the credit goes to whichever name sounds more like a starter, and that name gets the extra dollars whether the numbers earned them or not.

Start with what the site has already published. The September 2 camp report says Pittsburgh traded Kaleb Johnson to Green Bay, cut Travis Homer and Lew Nichols, and left Jaylen Warren and Rico Dowdle as the only pure running backs on the 53. It also says Dowdle was signed for early‑down and goal‑line work next to Warren, the passing‑down back. That report said to pay Warren like a clear lead back. The projection set does not agree, and here is why.

Warren is RB26 at 191.3 points. Dowdle is RB30 at 185.4. That is 5.9 points across a full season, about a third of a point a game. Warren catches more: 46 balls to 29, which is worth 17 points of reception credit before yardage. Dowdle runs more: 975 rushing yards to 791, and 5.5 rushing touchdowns to 4.5. Those two edges almost cancel, which is what a committee is. Neither one is the lead back. They are the lead back, cut in half.

Now add them up. Warren plus Dowdle is 376.7 points. The top back in this pool, Jahmyr Gibbs, is 364.7. Nobody can roster both halves of a split and play them in one slot, so the pair is not a Gibbs. But it tells you the Steelers have a full backfield’s worth of production to hand out, and the only question is how it gets divided. If the room prices that 376.7 as 191.3 for the famous half and 185.4 for the other, it should charge you almost the same for each. If it charges a lot more for the first half, the second half is the bargain.

Pittsburgh is not alone. New England has Stevenson and Henderson 5.2 points apart, and Carolina has Hubbard and Brooks 1.6 apart. Those are three committees in a row where the gap between the names is smaller than one good week from either back. The room treats the first name as the starter and the second as the insurance, and the pool says they are twins.

Why Dowdle and not Warren? Because the touchdowns and the yards point the same way. Dowdle has the early‑down and goal‑line job, and his 184 extra rushing yards and one extra rushing touchdown are worth about 24 points that do not vanish when a game script flips. Warren’s catches are real, but they are the part of a back’s year that depends most on Pittsburgh trailing. A back who gets the ball when the team leads has a floor. A back who gets it when the team trails has a hope.

The risk, out loud: these are projections, and a committee can break either way in a week. If Warren’s passing‑down role grows, he takes a bigger share and Dowdle shrinks to a touchdown vulture. Pittsburgh could also lean on one of them after an injury to the other, which flips the whole argument. And if your room already prices the two within a dollar of each other, there is no gap to buy. Check what the board says before you bid.

The line to take to your league: a committee is not a starter and a backup, it is two starters who each get half a season of the other one’s work. Do not pay a premium for the name that sounds like the lead.

The Pick: Rico Dowdle (PIT, RB), and bid him within a dollar or two of what Jaylen Warren (PIT, RB) costs. If the room leaves a bigger gap than that, take Dowdle and let somebody else pay the name premium.

Projected effect: +7% to +12% above his current price
