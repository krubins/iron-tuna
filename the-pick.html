The Pick

Nate Vega·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on draft night.

Build My Free Auction SheetThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

Jaxson Dart Is Already a Top‑Seven Quarterback on This Board, and the Market Is Still Pricing Him Like a Handcuff

Iron Tuna’s 2026 board has the rookie finishing at 297.2 points, the overall QB7 — ahead of Mahomes, Stafford, Lawrence, Prescott and Herbert — because his legs alone are worth nearly a third of his season, and none of theirs.

The standing rule for a rookie quarterback in year one is simple: assume nothing, price him like a lottery ticket, and let somebody else find out the hard way. Most years that rule is correct, because most rookie starters are exactly the mess it assumes. This year, applying it to one specific name on the Giants’ roster is about to cost somebody a real starting quarterback for backup money.

Run Iron Tuna’s 2026 projections at full PPR and Jaxson Dart finishes the season at 297.2 points, the overall QB7 — ahead of Patrick Mahomes (288.7), Matthew Stafford (288.4), Trevor Lawrence (285.6), Dak Prescott (283.9) and Justin Herbert (282.2). Five established veterans, a combined stack of Pro Bowl appearances between them, and a first‑year starter beats every one of them on this board.

The reason is not that Dart out‑throws anybody on that list. His 3,685 projected passing yards are the shallowest of the six by a wide margin — Stafford is projected for nearly 600 more. Split each quarterback’s total into what came from throwing and what came from running, and the case flips completely. Dart’s legs are worth 86.8 of his 297.2 points, 29.2% of his whole season. Mahomes leans on his legs for 16.1% of his own total. Stafford, who barely runs and is projected to lose a fumble or two on top of it, actually loses points on the ground — his rushing column is negative. Dart is not a better passer than any of the five veterans above. He is a good‑enough passer who also happens to be this year’s best rushing quarterback outside of Josh Allen and Lamar Jackson, and that combination is worth more than any of their arms alone.

The market has already half‑noticed. Our own September 3 quarterback board tagged Dart a “current market breakout,” calling him volatile but league‑winning if the rushing holds — good enough to earn a best‑ball target label, not good enough yet to be priced like a real starter. That is the gap this column is pointing at. A QB7 finish on this board is not a boom‑or‑bust flier; it is a real season‑long QB1 floor, still priced like a streamer because the market discounts every rookie starter by the same amount no matter what his legs already do for him. Meanwhile our own July 4 desk already flagged Mahomes as a reputation trap at a top‑tier price — the board says the same thing twice, once about a name you should stop overpaying and once about a name you have not started paying enough for.

Say the risk out loud, because a rookie calling every snap for the first time is not a sure thing just because the model likes him. His passing line is the shallowest of this group for a reason — this is a first‑year starter, and once opposing coordinators have a full season of tape, the roughest parts of his game get attacked first. A rushing‑heavy floor also means more hits, the same exposure this column already flagged for the running quarterbacks sitting behind Josh Allen. And Malik Nabers, the receiver this passing game leans on most, cleared the PUP list and looked healthy in our own August 27 camp notes — genuinely good news, but the team was still managing his workload carefully through the rest of camp, so a full, unrestricted season from him is encouraging, not guaranteed.

So stop pricing the rookie like a total unknown and start pricing him like the QB7 he already projects to be. A room that spends its last quarterback dollar on him because “he’s just a rookie” is the same room paying a premium two picks earlier for a passer this board already has him beating.

The Pick: Jaxson Dart (NYG, QB) at a real low‑end‑QB1 price, not the late‑round flier the market still treats him as — his legs alone are worth more than the gap separating him from the veterans above him. Do not pay a top‑ten premium for Patrick Mahomes (KC, QB) to sit one spot below him.

Projected effect: +15% to +22% above his current late‑round/streamer price

Theme: tier cliffsQBBUFSep 7

Josh Allen Is the Only Real Cliff at Quarterback. Everyone Below Him Is Standing on the Same Ledge.

Josh Allen’s drop to Lamar Jackson at QB2 costs 12.0% by itself. The next thirteen picks combined, all the way down to QB15, cost almost the same 13.3% — one real cliff at the very top, then a ledge that barely tilts.

Every draft room has a guy who treats the quarterback board like a subway map: evenly spaced stops, one price lower for every stop farther from first class. He watches QB2 come off the board, then QB3, then QB4, and pays a little less for each one like he is buying seats on the same train, just farther back. He is not wrong that quarterback has a top tier. He is wrong about where that tier ends, and this year it ends after exactly one player.

Run Iron Tuna’s 2026 projections at full PPR and Josh Allen finishes the season at 364.4 points, the clear QB1. Lamar Jackson is next at 320.8 — a real cliff, 12.0% of Allen’s own total gone in a single pick. So far the subway‑map theory holds: pay up for the top seat, expect the price to drop once you leave it. Then the theory breaks. Bo Nix, the QB8, is still sitting at 292.3, only 8.9% behind Lamar. Caleb Williams, the QB15, is at 278.2, another 4.8% down from there. Add those two drops together and the entire thirteen‑pick stretch from QB2 to QB15 costs 13.3% — barely more than the single cliff at the very top, and it took thirteen picks to get there instead of one.

The reason lives in the rushing column, and it explains why the cliff is one pick deep instead of five. Lamar Jackson actually out‑rushes Allen on paper this year — 671 projected yards to Allen’s 580 — and he is still 43.6 points behind him. Drake Maye (NE), Jalen Hurts (PHI) and Jayden Daniels (WAS) round out the next three slots, each with a real rushing floor of his own: 532, 438 and 670 yards. None of them touches Allen, because Allen is not winning on rushing volume alone — he is the only quarterback on the board pairing that kind of rushing total with a passing season that still ranks near the top of the position by itself. Everyone chasing him is trading a little passing for a little rushing, or the other way around, and once you are past him those trades keep netting out to roughly the same number, pick after pick after pick.

Put that 13.3% slide next to the identical stretch at the two positions this column has already covered this week. Running back loses 26.5% of Bijan Robinson’s value over the same thirteen picks, all the way down to Javonte Williams (DAL). Wide receiver loses 29.1%, from Ja’Marr Chase down to George Pickens (DAL). Quarterback loses less than half of either one over the identical stretch. In a one‑quarterback league, that is not a hunch about “waiting on QB” — it is the actual shape of this year’s board, and it is flatter at quarterback than it has any business being.

Say the risk out loud, because a ledge this flat is still standing on rushing legs. Four of the next five names below Allen — Lamar, Maye, Hurts, Daniels — are carrying real rushing floors, and a rushing floor is also a hit‑every‑week floor. Lose one of them for a month to the kind of injury a quarterback who runs the ball this much is simply more exposed to, and his single largest scoring lever goes down with him, with no deep passing tier underneath to catch him. Allen carries a version of that same exposure — he takes hits too — but Buffalo hired Joe Brady as head coach this year specifically to build a quicker, shorter passing game around him, per our own Play‑Caller Premium, so his passing floor does not lean on his legs the way the plateau below him does.

So stop pricing this position like a staircase. If Josh Allen is still on the board anywhere near a generic “elite QB1” price, that is underpricing the one real gap on the entire board — pay through it. Once he is gone, stop bidding like the position still has tiers left to protect. The ledge from QB2 to QB15 is nearly flat, so let the room fight over Lamar Jackson and Drake Maye at a premium, and take whoever is left near the QB12‑to‑QB15 turn for a fraction of the price and almost the same season.

The Pick: Josh Allen (BUF, QB) at a real premium over the going “elite QB1” rate — the gap he protects is bigger than most rooms actually charge for it. Once he is off the board, stop paying quarterback prices at all until the QB12‑to‑QB15 turn, where Caleb Williams (CHI, QB) projects within 13.3% of the QB2 money everyone else just overpaid for.

Projected effect: +8% to +14% above his current QB1 price

Theme: the shape of the moneyRBDETSep 6

Running Backs Bleed Value Every Round. Wide Receivers Bleed It Once, Then Stop.

From RB12 to RB36 on Iron Tuna’s 2026 board, the position gives up 38.5% of its points. Wide receiver gives up 25.6% over the identical two‑dozen‑pick stretch. Same auction, two completely different shapes.

Every draft forum has the same argument on a loop every August: stars‑and‑scrubs versus balanced, spend big early or spread it around, and it never actually resolves because both sides are arguing from vibes. Nobody pulls up the board and asks the only question that matters — not “which strategy sounds smarter,” but “which position actually keeps losing value the deeper you go, and which one stops?” Run the numbers instead of the debate, and the two positions turn out to have completely different shapes, which means they deserve completely different strategies.

Run Iron Tuna’s 2026 projections at full PPR and running back falls off a cliff, catches its breath, and falls off another one. Jahmyr Gibbs is the RB1 at 364.7 points. Breece Hall, the RB12, is already down to 271.3 — a 25.6% drop. Keep going and the position does not level out: TreVeyon Henderson, the RB24, is down to 196.8, another 27.5% gone, which means the RB1 has lost 46.0% of his value by the time you reach the RB24 turn. Two cliffs, back to back, no plateau between them.

But then the position stops falling apart. Rome Odunze, the WR24, is down to 211.1, just 14.8% further. Parker Washington, the WR36, is down to 184.4, another 12.6%. Line the two positions up over the identical stretch — slot 12 to slot 36, two full dozen picks — and running back gives up 38.5% of Breece Hall’s value while wide receiver gives up 25.6% of Garrett Wilson’s. The receiver position takes its punch early and mostly stands back up. The running back position just keeps getting hit.

The mechanism is touches versus targets. A true bell‑cow running back hoards a whole backfield’s workload almost by himself, so every step down the board is a step toward a committee that splits the same touches three or four ways — there is no floor to hit until you are all the way down at handcuff money. Detroit traded David Montgomery away and handed Gibbs the entire job, which is exactly why he sits alone at the top of that first table with nobody in his own backfield left to leak touches to. Receiver works the opposite way: an offense throws to five or six bodies over a season no matter who is No. 1, so a cleared‑out depth chart creates real weekly volume several names deep, not just for the top target. Chicago traded DJ Moore away this offseason, and our own auction desk already flagged Odunze as the clear inheritor of those vacated looks — the same trade logic that made Gibbs untouchable at running back instead widens the receiver shelf, because the looks land on the second or third man down the depth chart just as easily as the first.

Say the risk out loud in both directions. Gibbs’s workload is only bulletproof as long as Isiah Pacheco stays the clean handcuff our own camp notes describe him as; a real injury to Gibbs himself would prove the position’s concentration cuts both ways. And the receiver shelf is flat this year partly because two separate depth charts just got cleared out by trades — a normal year without that much roster churn would show a smaller gap between the two positions than this one does.

So stop re‑litigating stars‑and‑scrubs in the abstract and let the shape of this year’s board decide it position by position. At running back, where the cliffs never stop, pay all the way up for the true workhorse and do not talk yourself into saving money on the way down — there is no shelf to land on. At receiver, where the fall is front‑loaded, let the room pay the WR12 premium and buy off the flat shelf instead; you are giving up far less than the running back board would have charged you for the same patience.

The Pick: Jahmyr Gibbs (DET, RB) is the one running back worth paying all the way up for — his position has no plateau to hide behind, so the discipline is to keep bidding, not to wait for one. Rome Odunze (CHI, WR) is the flat shelf’s proof: 211.1 points at a WR24 price that is only 14.8% behind the WR12 line above him, with the vacated Chicago target tree our own desk already likes at a discount.

Projected effect: +10% to +16% above his current WR2-tier price

Theme: touchdown regressionRBPHISep 5

Saquon Barkley, Kenneth Walker and Derrick Henry Are Tied on the Scoreboard, and Only One of Them Needs the Touchdowns to Show Up

Iron Tuna’s 2026 board has all three within six‑tenths of a point of each other. Henry needs touchdowns for 31.4% of his total; Barkley and Walker need them for under a quarter.

Every draft room has a guy who believes touchdowns are a skill, the same way he believes free throws are a skill: repeatable, learnable, something a player simply has. It is a comforting idea and it is mostly wrong. Yards are a skill — you earn them, snap after snap, against whichever front shows up. A touchdown is mostly a coincidence that happens to a player who was already standing near the goal line when the play was called, and coincidences do not clock in on schedule for seventeen straight weeks. This happens to a great running back almost every year: someone scores twenty‑plus total touchdowns on a monster workload, gets paid for it again the following August, and comes back with fifteen on almost the identical workload, because nobody — not him, not his coordinator — actually controls how many of his carries start on the eight‑yard line. Three backs on this year’s board are about to find out which side of that coin they are standing on, because they are projected for virtually the same season, built two completely different ways.

Run Iron Tuna’s 2026 projections and Saquon Barkley finishes at 272.6 points. Kenneth Walker III finishes at 272.6 as well — an exact tie. Derrick Henry finishes at 273.2, six‑tenths of a point above both of them. Split each total into the points that came from a touchdown and the points that came from everything else, and the tie stops looking like a tie. Henry’s 13.1 projected rushing touchdowns and 1.2 receiving touchdowns are worth 85.8 of his 273.2 points — 31.4% of his entire season riding on the ball crossing the goal line. Barkley’s touchdowns are worth 66.0 of his 272.6, 24.2%. Walker’s are worth 64.8 of his 272.6, 23.8%. Same shelf, same neighborhood on the board, and one of the three needs seven more points of every hundred to come from the single least repeatable stat in the sport.

The touch sheet explains the gap. Barkley is projected for 1,285 rushing yards and 43 catches; Walker for 1,239 rushing yards and 48 catches. Both are built on the two components of a running back’s game that show up almost as reliably as they are projected — a workhorse’s rushing yardage and a real role in the passing game, the exact kind of target share this column has already made the case for at receiver. Henry is projected for more rushing yards than either of them, 1,484, but only 21 catches, a third-down role Baltimore has never really given him. Take away his touchdown rate and there is almost nothing left underneath it to catch him — no receiving floor, no second column of production to lean on if the scores dry up for a month.

Say the risk out loud in both directions, because a column that only flatters its own argument is a press release. Henry’s touchdown rate is not pure luck — he is a 240‑pound closer who has finished inside the five‑yard line more often than most backs in the league for a decade, and Baltimore’s offensive line still clears the same lanes it always has. But he also shares that red zone with Lamar Jackson, whose legs are the first thing this offense runs at the goal line, which is exactly the kind of competition for scores that a pure rushing‑yardage projection cannot fully price in. Barkley and Walker carry their own risk, too: a new play‑caller in either building could shrink the receiving role the projection is counting on. Neither risk is nothing. But only one of these three players needs his best month of touchdown luck to repeat itself just to stay tied with the other two.

So price the tie correctly instead of treating “272 projected points” as one number. A running back whose season leans on catches and rushing yards survives a quiet month at the goal line with his ranking mostly intact. A running back whose season leans on touchdowns does not — he just has a worse month, and the room that paid for the highlight reel finds out why the spreadsheet was warning them the whole time.

The Pick: Saquon Barkley (PHI, RB), whose 272.6 points lean on the sturdier half of the ledger — pay it in full, and do not let a room talk you into a discount just because he lacks Henry’s highlight reel. Build a small discount into Derrick Henry’s (BAL, RB) matching price instead; his total needs nearly a third of itself to come from the single stat that swings hardest from one season to the next.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% above his current committee-proof price

Theme: the cost of an injury handcuffRBCARSep 4

Two Handcuffs Cost the Same Dollar, and Only One of Them Is Actually a Handcuff

James Conner loses two‑thirds of Jeremiyah Love’s value the moment he takes over Arizona’s backfield. Jonathon Brooks loses less than one percent of Chuba Hubbard’s. Both go for the same crumpled dollar at the end of your auction.

Every draft room has a five‑minute stretch, right near the end, where the nominations turn into a garage sale. Backup running backs fly across the table for a dollar apiece, and the room treats every single one of them like the same bet: a lottery ticket that pays off only if a star gets carted off. Nobody stops to ask whether the ticket is actually worth anything if it hits. Two of this year’s cheapest tickets prove that assumption wrong in opposite directions, on the same day, at the same position.

Run Iron Tuna’s 2026 projections on Arizona’s backfield and Jeremiyah Love finishes the season at 276.8 points. James Conner, the veteran ticketed to spell him, projects for 93.6 — a 183.2‑point collapse, 66.2% of Love’s own total gone the moment Conner has to be the lead back instead of the caddy. That is what a real handcuff looks like: cheap because the offense actually falls off a cliff without the starter. Now run Carolina’s backfield the same way. Chuba Hubbard projects for 171.5 points. Jonathon Brooks, the guy sitting behind him at the same one‑dollar price, projects for 169.9 — a gap of 1.6 points across an entire season. Not a handcuff. A photocopy.

The reason lives in the touch sheet, not just the final score. Love is projected as a true 300‑touch bell‑cow — 1,128 rushing yards and 65 catches — while Conner and Allgeier split whatever is left of a backfield that was never built to share. Carolina is the opposite setup by design. Brooks projects for more rushing yards than Hubbard does, 810 to 709, and only eight fewer catches, 33 to 41. Our own camp notes from September 1 have Carolina keeping all three backs on its initial roster, with Hubbard still nursing a lingering hamstring strain and Brooks — fully healthy after his own long recovery — already the one who steps in if Hubbard needs more time. The model is not guessing at a timeshare. The coaching staff already built one.

Say the risk out loud, because it cuts toward Brooks, not away from him. If Hubbard’s hamstring holds up clean for seventeen games and the staff never needs to manage his workload, Brooks could see fewer defined touches than the model assumes and land short of 169.9. But that is a bet on a running back with an active soft‑tissue injury playing every down of a full season on a team that has already shown it is comfortable using both backs. The safer read is that Carolina protects Hubbard by design, Brooks keeps eating the touches that decision creates, and the two backs keep finishing within a rounding error of each other the way the projections already say they will.

So stop bidding on labels and start bidding on the number. A dollar is the right price for Tyler Allgeier, because his own line says he is worth about a dollar if Love goes down. It is the wrong price for Jonathon Brooks, whose line already says he is worth almost exactly what the $30‑plus starter in front of him is worth. Pay for the real handcuff at the real handcuff price, and pay for the photocopy like the running back he already projects to be.

The Pick: Jonathon Brooks (CAR, RB) at a real RB2 price, not garage‑sale money — the projections already have him within 1% of the man he is supposedly just insuring. Do not extend the same generosity to James Conner (ARI, RB); his own 93.6‑point line proves he is exactly the one‑dollar lottery ticket the room assumes every backup is.

Projected effect: +25% to +35% above his current handcuff price

Theme: positional scarcityTEARISep 3

Trey McBride and Brock Bowers Are Tight End’s Two Penthouses, and Everyone Else Is Splitting One Studio

Iron Tuna’s 2026 board has a 12.6% cliff between the TE2 seat and the TE3 seat — more than four times the size of the same seat‑to‑seat drop at running back or receiver. Pay for the top two, or skip the middle entirely.

Every auction room treats a position ranking like a staircase: pay a little less for each step down, evenly spaced, forever, all the way to the basement. That works fine at receiver. It mostly works at running back. At tight end this year it is a lie your own cheat sheet is telling you, because the position is not a staircase. It is a two‑seat balcony bolted to the side of a building, and underneath it there is nothing but a long, flat parking lot.

Run Iron Tuna’s 2026 projections at full PPR and Trey McBride finishes the season at 241.1 points, the overall TE1. Brock Bowers sits right behind him at 240.4, a rounding error apart. Then the floor drops out: Tyler Warren, the TE3, projects for 210.0 — a 30.4‑point fall in one spot, 12.6% of Bowers’s own total gone in a single tier change. Run the identical seat‑to‑seat math at running back (Bijan Robinson’s 351.6 down to Christian McCaffrey’s 341.3, a 2.9% drop) or receiver (Ja’Marr Chase’s 336.6 down to Jaxon Smith‑Njigba’s 326.2, a 3.1% drop), and the tight end cliff is more than four times as steep, as a share of the points at stake, as the same move at either other skill position.

The reason shows up in the target sheet, not just the final score. McBride is projected for 108 catches and 1,023 yards — wide receiver production out of the tight end slot, the way our own auction desk put it back in July. Bowers checks in at 99 catches and 999 yards of his own. Warren, a real player having a real second‑year leap in Indianapolis, still projects 23 fewer catches and 129 fewer yards than Bowers. He is good. He is not that.

Below the top two, the position stops discriminating. Travis Kelce at TE10 (177.3) and Brenton Strange at TE20 (142.1) are separated by 35.2 points across ten full ranks of the board — barely more than the single gap between Bowers and Warren all by itself. A tight end taken TE12 is not meaningfully different from one taken TE19; you are not buying a player at that price, you are buying a coin flip on which bye week hurts less.

Say the risk out loud instead of burying it. Arizona released Kyler Murray and handed the offense to Jacoby Brissett, a downgrade for nearly every skill player in that building except McBride, whose catch radius makes him the safety valve no matter who is throwing him the ball — our own Play‑Caller Premium made exactly that case, and the tight end board backs it up. Bowers carries a version of the same protection in Las Vegas, where Klint Kubiak’s scheme runs its easiest completions right through where he already wins. Warren’s gap is not a knock on him; it is what “the position has two seats” looks like when a good player is standing just outside the door.

So do the arithmetic before the room does. If McBride or Bowers is still on the board near his going rate, that is the bid worth stretching for — not the TE6 or TE8 a room will talk itself into as “good value.” And if you are priced out of both seats, do not split the difference on a $20 tight end who scores like a $9 one. Skip straight to the flat shelf at the bottom, grab whichever name is cheapest that week, and spend the saved dollar somewhere the staircase is still actually a staircase.

The Pick: Trey McBride (ARI, TE), the safer of tight end’s two seats now that a downgraded Arizona passing game makes his catch radius even more valuable, not less. Brock Bowers (LV, TE) is the same bet from the other seat — pay one of the two, or skip the position’s entire middle tier and grab a name off the TE12‑to‑TE20 shelf instead.

Projected effect: +14% to +20% above his current TE1 price

Theme: target concentrationWRNOSep 2

Chris Olave Owns a Smaller Slice of New Orleans Than Zay Flowers Owns of Baltimore — and Still Outscores Him by 11.5 Points

Target share tells you who won the job. It does not tell you how big the job is, and New Orleans throws the ball around a lot more than Baltimore does.

Every draft room has a guy who reads one stat and stops reading. He finds a receiver who eats 36% of his team’s targets, calls him a “target hog,” and pays a premium like he just found the building’s sole landlord. Nobody asks how big the building is. Sometimes the landlord owns a shed.

Run Iron Tuna’s 2026 projections and Zay Flowers is that landlord. He is pencilled in for 82 of Baltimore’s 227 projected catches among wideouts and tight ends — a 36.1% share, actually a hair bigger than Justin Jefferson’s 35.6% share of Minnesota’s targets. Same monopoly, roughly. Jefferson finishes the season at 293.3 points, the WR5. Flowers finishes at 238.2, the WR16. A 55‑point gap between two receivers who each own about a third of their team’s passing-game catches.

The math is not complicated once you see it: a share is a fraction, and a fraction is only as good as its denominator. Baltimore’s receiving corps is projected for 227 catches all season, because Derrick Henry and this offense would rather hand it off — the same run‑first identity that made Henry a standard‑league RB5 in this column two weeks ago. Minnesota’s corps catches 312 balls, 85 more, because the Vikings actually throw it. Own a third of a small pie and you still have a small plate.

Chris Olave is the receipt on the other side of the ledger. His 27.7% share looks unremarkable next to Flowers’s 36.1% — a smaller slice, full stop, the number a spreadsheet skimmer discounts him for. But New Orleans’s receiving corps is projected for 328 catches, the most of any team in this table, Puka Nacua’s Rams included. A smaller cut of a bigger number still nets Olave 91 catches and 249.7 points, the WR9 — ahead of Flowers’s WR16 finish despite the “worse” share.

The monopoly is real, too, not just a projection artifact. Our own camp notes from August 28 have Jordyn Tyson’s hamstring injury opening the door for Olave as the unquestioned top target in New Orleans for at least the first month of the season, with Devaughn Vele the only other name drawing snaps behind him. That is exactly the shape of a share worth paying for — a real vacancy, sitting inside a passing offense that already throws more than almost anyone else on this list.

Say the risk out loud, because a column that only says nice things is a press release. Tyson comes back from that hamstring eventually, and every game he plays trims Olave’s slice back down. Flowers is not a bust, either — a Ravens team that falls behind on the scoreboard throws more than this projection assumes, and he already owns the route tree for when that happens. Neither risk changes the number sitting in front of you right now: one of these receivers is priced like a monopoly and is actually running a small shop, and the other is priced like a discount and is actually running the biggest store in the mall.

Do the arithmetic before you pay the “target hog” premium. Multiply the share by the pool it is a share of, not just the percentage on the sheet.

The Pick: Chris Olave (NO, WR) at the discount a 27.7% share earns him on a skim, before the market notices the 328-catch pool it is 27.7% of. Do not pay Flowers-level money for Zay Flowers (BAL, WR) on target share alone — his monopoly is real, but it is a monopoly on a shed.

Projected effect: +10% to +16% above his current WR2-tier price

Theme: committee backfieldsRBPITSep 1

Pittsburgh’s Backfield Is a Photo Finish, and Your League Is Still Betting on One Horse

Jaylen Warren and Rico Dowdle project 5.9 points apart over a full season — a coin flip wearing shoulder pads — and Pittsburgh is not even the closest committee on the board.

Every summer, a beat reporter stands at the practice fence and files some version of the same sentence: both backs are getting first‑team reps, and the staff loves what each of them brings. That sentence gets written so often it should have its own keyboard shortcut. It is not news. It is a coaching staff declining to do a fantasy manager’s job for them, on purpose, for as long as they can get away with it.

Run Iron Tuna’s 2026 projections and the reason for the stalling shows up in the math. Jaylen Warren finishes the season at 191.3 points; Rico Dowdle finishes at 185.4. That is 5.9 points over seventeen games — a third of a point a week, the kind of gap one lucky fumble recovery erases. Warren gets the third‑down work, 46 projected catches to Dowdle’s 29; Dowdle gets the early‑down and goal‑line carries. The model can only project what the depth chart tells it, and right now the depth chart is telling it nothing.

Pittsburgh is not even the tightest race. New England’s Rhamondre Stevenson and TreVeyon Henderson sit 5.2 points apart — 202.0 to 196.8, a gap so small it is really a rounding error wearing a helmet. Two different teams, two different coaching staffs, the same math. When a backfield is this close on paper, it usually means the coaches genuinely have not decided either, and your draft room — which loves a decisive board — is about to invent a decision that does not exist yet.

Here is the part that should worry you before it comforts you: close backfields do not stay close. They resolve, and usually all at once. New Orleans had exactly this kind of committee in July — Alvin Kamara and Travis Etienne within shouting distance of each other on paper — until Kamara sprained his MCL in an August practice and Etienne inherited the job outright. The same kind of math that once had them nearly tied now has them 128.4 points apart. That is what a resolved committee looks like in the numbers, and it is worth remembering every time a five‑point gap feels stable. It is not calm water. It is water that has not decided which way to flow yet.

So do not pay a starter’s price for either half of a true committee, and if you have to pick a side, pick the one who catches the ball. Warren’s 46 projected catches are the reason his 191.3 survives more versions of the season than Dowdle’s 185.4 does — even if Pittsburgh eventually leans on one back for early downs, the receiving role tends to be stickier, because it is the job a passing‑down back keeps even after he loses carries. One town over, the same bet is already paying off: New England’s own camp reports have Henderson’s snap share climbing on Stevenson, which is exactly the shape a resolving committee takes before the box score admits it.

The Pick: Jaylen Warren (PIT, RB) at the discount a true committee back gets, not the discount a healthy scratch gets — his catch role is the safer half of Pittsburgh’s coin flip. If you would rather buy the side that has already started to move, TreVeyon Henderson (NE, RB) is the back whose camp usage is trending up on a price that has not caught up yet.

Projected effect: +9% to +15% above his current committee-back price

Theme: scoring settingsRBBALAug 20

Your league’s scoring setting moves Derrick Henry four spots, and half your room has not opened that page since 2019

The same projections produce a different board depending on what a catch is worth. Nobody prices for it, which is where the money is.

Every fantasy league has one settings page and one guy who has read it. Be that guy. It takes ninety seconds and it is worth more than any sleeper list you will read this month, because the receptions line at the top of that page silently reorders your entire running back board before a single bid is made.

Run our projections at full PPR and Derrick Henry is the RB9, 273.2 points, behind a pile of pass‑catching backs. Run the exact same projections at standard scoring — no point per catch, nothing else changed — and Henry is the RB5 at 252.2, because he only loses the 21 catches he was never getting anyway. Four spots. Same player, same season, same 1,484 rushing yards and thirteen touchdowns. The only thing that moved was a checkbox.

Notice that it cuts both ways, which is what makes it a theme and not a hot take. Jeremiyah Love is our RB8 in full PPR and our RB13 in standard. Ashton Jeanty goes 6 to 12. Those are not small moves, and they are moving in the opposite direction from Henry, which means in a standard league the room is doing the exact wrong thing twice at the same time: paying up for the catches that no longer count, and discounting the 300‑touch back whose entire profile just got more valuable.

The same four hold the top of the board in every format — Jahmyr Gibbs, Bijan Robinson, Christian McCaffrey and Jonathan Taylor take RB1 through RB4 whatever a catch is worth, because they are good at both halves of the job. Elite is elite. It is the RB5‑to‑RB18 band, the part of the board where most of your money actually goes, that shuffles like a deck of cards.

The practical version, since that is the point of all this. In a full PPR league, do not let somebody else’s Henry price talk you out of Love or Jeanty; the catches are real points and you should pay for them. In a half‑PPR or standard league, sit on your hands while the room drafts receptions, and then take the projected top‑five back who is going at RB9 money.

The Pick: Derrick Henry (BAL, RB) in any league that is not full PPR — a top‑five back at his scoring setting, priced by a room that is still reading a PPR ranking list. In full PPR, flip the entry over and buy Jeremiyah Love (ARI, RB) instead.

Projected effect: +15% to +22% above his price in standard and half-PPR scoring
