The Pick

The Pick

Market Desk·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on your lineup, your waiver claim and your draft board alike.

This week’s boardThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

The column
Theme: positional scarcityTEARINov 22

Tight End Scarcity Is Two Players Deep. Pay for Trey McBride or Pay a Dollar, Because the Middle Is a Waiting Room.

Trey McBride and Brock Bowers each project to beat the 12th tight end by more than 71 points. The seven tight ends ranked TE5 to TE11 sit within 11.5 points of each other, less than a point a game. At tight end the scarce thing is two players, not the position.

Airlines board in groups. Group 1 is a few people with a fancy card. Then there is Group 5, which is everybody else, standing in a crowd by the gate, pretending to be Group 3. Nobody in Group 5 gets on the plane sooner by standing closer to the door. Hold that crowd at the gate. It comes back.

Tight end is the position fantasy players call “scarce,” and the site’s projections say that is half true. Measure each tight end against the 12th one, the starter a 12‑team league is left with when everyone has one, and the board splits in two. McBride projects to 241.1 points, 72.0 more than Mark Andrews at TE12. Bowers is 240.4, 71.3 more. Then there is a 30.4‑point drop to Tyler Warren at TE3. After Colston Loveland at TE4, the rest of the starters fall into one crowd: Harold Fannin Jr. at 188.5 down to Dallas Goedert at 177.0. Seven players, 11.5 points, 0.7 a game.

Now put those gaps next to the other positions, measured the same way against each one’s last starter: RB24 and WR36 in a 12‑team league. McBride’s 72.0 is about what Saquon Barkley at RB10 (75.8) and Rashee Rice at WR8 (75.6) give you over their own replacements. So McBride is not a luxury. He is a top‑ten running back who happens to line up next to the tackle. Kittle’s 18.7 is about what Carnell Tate at WR29 gives you, 18.4. Nobody pays a star price for WR29. People do it at tight end every year, because “TE7” sounds like a starter and “the 29th receiver” sounds like a bench.

The mechanism is catches. McBride projects to 108 of them and Bowers to 99. No other tight end reaches 86. A tight end’s points are mostly volume, and only two of them get receiver volume. Everyone from TE5 to TE11 is between 71 and 84 catches, which is why they score the same. Our July 30 insight drop argued that Arizona’s switch to Jacoby Brissett actually helps McBride, because a short passing game feeds the middle of the field, and the Play‑Caller Premium says the same about mismatch tight ends outlasting bad quarterbacks. We agree with both, and this table is why it matters: his floor is the thing the position does not have.

And the cheap end is not a punt. Our August 17 drop called Goedert a direct beneficiary of the A.J. Brown trade. He is already TE11, and he trails Kittle by 10.8 points, 0.6 a game. If the extra targets show up, he joins the crowd ahead of him for a fraction of the cost. Our camp desk’s September 8 report said to price Kittle as a healthy, full‑workload starter, and we are not arguing with that. A healthy Kittle is exactly what is on this sheet, and a healthy Kittle is still in the crowd.

The risk, out loud: touchdowns can close a gap that catches open. McBride projects to only 5.3 of them, and a tight end in the crowd who scores like Andrews does (7.1) would climb out of it. The top two are also the two most expensive tight ends on the board, so paying the full price for one eats money you would otherwise spend on a running back. That is the trade: a top‑ten running back’s edge, bought at tight end. And in a league that starts two tight ends, or pays a bonus for tight end catches, the crowd thins out and every rank in it costs more. Back to the gate. Either board with Group 1 or wait comfortably in your seat for Group 5. Standing by the door with a TE7 ticket gets you on the same plane, just poorer.

The Pick: Trey McBride (ARI, TE). Bid him like the top‑ten running back his 72.0‑point edge says he is, and do not let the word “tight end” scare you off the price. If you miss on him and Bowers, skip the TE5 to TE10 bidding war and take Dallas Goedert (PHI, TE) for a dollar or two at the end.

Projected effect: +8% to +14% above his current price

Theme: rookie pricingRBSEANov 21

Jadarian Price Runs Like a Starter and Gets Priced Like a Rookie. The Rookie Part Is Already in the Number.

Jadarian Price projects to 139.6 rushing points, tied for the most of any back from RB21 to RB30. He ranks RB25 only because of 27 catches, and the projection already pays Zach Charbonnet 102.0 points for coming back. Taking a rookie discount off that number takes it twice.

Every gym class has the new kid. Nobody knows him, so he gets picked ninth, right after the kid who brought a note from his mom. Then the game starts and he turns out to be the fastest person in the building. The captains are not stupid. They just priced “new” instead of “fast.” Remember the new kid. He comes back.

Fantasy rooms do the same thing to rookie running backs who were not a top‑five pick. Seattle took Price with the last pick of the first round, and our camp desk has called him a buy since July 21. Here is what the site’s projections say he is. Price projects to 922 rushing yards and 7.9 rushing touchdowns, which is 139.6 points from running alone. From RB20 to RB30 on this board, only D’Andre Swift, at 148.0, gets more out of the run game. Bhayshul Tuten ties him. Bucky Irving, David Montgomery and Tony Pollard, all ranked around him or above him, get less.

So why is he RB25 and not RB21? Catches. His 27 catches are worth 56.0 points, and Irving’s 37 are worth 76.6. That is the whole gap and then some. It is also the part of a rookie back’s game most likely to grow, because a coach gives a rookie the third‑down job last, once he trusts him to block. Give Price 10 more catches at his own 7.9 yards a catch and he is at about 211, which is RB20. He does not need more carries to get there. He needs a coach to stop worrying.

Now the double discount. The usual reason to knock a rookie down is that the veteran will take his job back. In Seattle that veteran is Zach Charbonnet, and the projection already gives Charbonnet 102.0 points, 424 rushing yards and 5.3 rushing touchdowns. That is a back who comes back and plays. Price still owns 922 of the 1,394 rushing yards Seattle’s three backs project to, 66.1% of them. His 193.6 is the number with Charbonnet in it, not the number without him. Our August 31 camp report said to price Price as a real Week 1 starter, not a handcuff, and the Play‑Caller Premium’s September 7 call said the same thing. We agree with both, and this is the reason in one line: the room’s worry is already printed on the sheet.

Where this sits with the rookie at the top of the class: Jeremiyah Love projects to 276.8 as the RB8, and the site has told you to price him down for his high ankle sprain, not up for being the No. 3 pick. We are not arguing with that call. It is the same rule pointed the other way. Price the rookie for what the sheet says, not for where he was drafted.

The risk, out loud: Price’s 7.9 rushing touchdowns are 58.1% of the backfield’s, and touchdowns wander. If Charbonnet comes back healthier than the August reports said, his share could be bigger than 102.0. And the catches may simply never come, which leaves Price as an early‑down back with an RB25 ceiling for this year. Back to gym class. You do not have to pick the new kid first. Just stop picking him ninth when the sheet already says he is fast.

The Pick: Jadarian Price (SEA, RB). Bid him at his full RB25 price, with no rookie discount on top, and treat any catches he adds as free upside toward RB20. Keep Zach Charbonnet (SEA, RB) at the cheap‑stash price our camp desk set, and do not pay him like the starter he was before the knee.

Projected effect: +8% to +14% above a rookie-discount price

Theme: committee backfieldsRBTBNov 20

Tampa Bay’s Backfield Isn’t Small. It’s Sliced. Bucky Irving Holds the Bigger Slice of a Pie Bigger Than Baltimore’s.

Bucky Irving and Kenneth Gainwell project to 395.5 points between them, the fifth‑richest backfield in the pool and 8.3 points more than Derrick Henry and Justice Hill. Irving’s 52.0% of it makes him RB21, and every five points of share is worth about 20 points.

Nobody has ever opened a large pizza, seen eight slices and said, “Wow, small pizza.” Fantasy drafters do it every year with running backs. They hear the word “committee,” picture two guys fighting over scraps and bid accordingly. Hold that pizza. It comes back.

Here is what the scraps look like in this site’s projections. Add up each team’s top two running backs. Detroit, Atlanta and San Francisco lead, because they have Jahmyr Gibbs, Bijan Robinson and Christian McCaffrey. Fourth is New England at 398.8. Fifth is Tampa Bay at 395.5. Both of those are committees. Baltimore, with Henry, is seventh at 387.2. Arizona, with Jeremiyah Love, is tenth at 370.4. Buffalo, with James Cook, is twelfth at 363.4. Chicago and Pittsburgh, two more committees, are eighth and ninth. Four of the nine richest backfields in the league are the ones the room calls crowded.

So the committee does not make Tampa’s pie small. It makes each slice depend on the split, and the split is where the money is. Irving holds 52.0% of 395.5 and projects to 205.5, RB21. Move his share to 60%, close to the 59.2% Kyren Williams gets of the Rams’ backfield, and he is 237.3, RB17. Drop it to 45% and he is 178.0, RB30. Five points of share in a pie this size is worth 19.8 points. The same five points in Denver’s 322.9 backfield is worth 16.1. A big pie makes every argument about the split a bigger argument.

Why bet on the split moving toward Irving and not away? Look at what each back’s points are made of. Gainwell’s 59 catches and 433 receiving yards are worth 120.3 of his 190.0. That is a passing‑down job, and the Play‑Caller Premium’s September 7 call says it is a safe one no matter who gets the carries. We agree, and it cuts both ways: if Gainwell’s job is fixed, the part of this backfield that can still move is the carries. Irving owns 991 of the 1,710 rushing yards Tampa’s three backs project to, 58.0% of them, and his rushing alone is worth 130.9 points. The slice that can grow is his.

Where this sits with the rest of the site: our July 2 camp report called Irving the clear anchor and the only Tampa back worth real auction money, and said to pay him as a solid RB2 and stop short of a bell‑cow bid. That is exactly the range this table prints, RB17 to RB21. The June 22 report said to discount Irving off his old price while his shoulder healed and to grab Gainwell cheap. We agree with both. What we do not agree with is discounting him twice: once for the shoulder and again for the word “committee.”

The risk, out loud: Irving is coming off shoulder surgery, and that June report said Tampa was not sure he would be ready before summer or fall. Sean Tucker makes it a three‑man room, and a third mouth takes bites out of both slices. The split can also go the other way, and 45% is RB30. And the pie itself is a projection; Baker Mayfield projects as only the QB27, so if Tampa’s offense stalls, every slice shrinks. Back to the pizza. Nobody is asking you to eat the whole thing. Just stop paying slice prices for the biggest slice of one of the biggest pies on the table.

The Pick: Bucky Irving (TB, RB). Bid him as the solid RB2 our camp desk called him, priced at RB21 with an RB17 ceiling, and do not knock him down a tier because his team uses two backs. For a dollar or two, Kenneth Gainwell (TB, RB) is the other half of the same pie and the receiving job that keeps it big.

Projected effect: +8% to +14% above a committee-back price
