The Pick

The Pick

Market Desk·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on your lineup, your waiver claim and your draft board alike.

This week’s boardThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

The column
Theme: the cost of an injury handcuffRBDETNov 24

Most Handcuffs Are a Spare‑Tire Well With No Tire in It. Isiah Pacheco Is a Full‑Size Spare Bolted to the Best Car on the Lot.

Isiah Pacheco owns 26.3% of Detroit’s projected running‑back rushing yards, the biggest share of any backup in the table below, and the starter he backs up, Jahmyr Gibbs, is the RB1. A handcuff is insurance, and his is the biggest policy that also pays while nothing goes wrong.

Open the trunk of a used car and you find one of three things. A full‑size spare, which gets you home. A donut, which gets you to a shop at 50 miles an hour while everyone passes you. Or the little foam cutout shaped like a tire, with no tire in it, which gets you a long talk with a tow truck driver. All three cost the same at the dealership, because nobody opens the trunk. Remember the foam cutout. It comes back.

A handcuff is a policy on your best running back, so judge it like a policy: how big is the claim, and what do you get while you wait for one? On the first, nobody beats Detroit. The site’s projections make Gibbs the RB1 at 364.7 points, 21.5 a game. That is the biggest weekly hole in the running‑back pool if he misses time. On the second, look at what the backup already does with the starter healthy. Pacheco projects to 489 rushing yards and 3.4 rushing touchdowns, which is 69.3 points from running alone and 26.3% of the 1,862 rushing yards Detroit’s backs project to. He is not a note on the depth chart. He has a job.

Why rushing share and not total points? Because a policy pays out in the job you lose, not the job you keep. Justice Hill projects to more points than Pacheco, 114.0 to 93.8, but 39 catches make up most of that. He gets 223 rushing yards to Derrick Henry’s 1,484. If Henry goes down, the sheet has Hill as the passing‑down back, not the man already taking early‑down carries. That is a donut. It works, but it is not the tire you had. Pacheco is already doing the starter’s kind of work, just less of it, so when he gets more of it nobody has to guess who gets the ball.

And then there is Indianapolis. Jonathan Taylor is the RB4 at 315.6 points, and the best backup in the pool behind him is Seth McGowan at 15.8, with 52 rushing yards. That is the foam cutout. The projection is not saying McGowan inherits 1,500 yards if Taylor gets hurt. It is saying nobody knows who does, and a handcuff you cannot name is just a bench spot you paid for.

Where this sits with the rest of the site. Our August 27 camp report said to keep Pacheco priced as a real handcuff to Gibbs and not discount him for his camp MCL sprain. The Play‑Caller Premium’s August 22 call went further and said Dan Campbell’s fourth‑down habit makes Pacheco a touchdown source even with Gibbs healthy. We agree with both. Our August 18 camp report called him a one‑to‑two dollar handcuff, not a standalone play, and the sheet agrees he is not a weekly starter at RB51. Our only quarrel is the price tag. The best policy in the table should not cost the same as the foam cutout.

The risk, out loud: insurance only pays if something happens, and a healthy Gibbs leaves Pacheco at about 5.5 points a game, which will not crack your lineup. Pacheco’s 3.4 rushing touchdowns are 19.0% of Detroit’s backfield total, and touchdowns wander. And a handcuff is worth the most to the person who owns the starter. To everyone else he is a lottery ticket, so price him by whose roster he is going to. Back to the trunk. With the fantasy playoffs close, the Gibbs manager is the one driver who should check the spare before the trip, because his car is the one everyone else would steal the tire from.

The Pick: Isiah Pacheco (DET, RB). If you roster Gibbs, he is the one handcuff in the league worth a real bid or a real waiver claim, not the minimum. If you do not, grab him anyway as the biggest payout on the board if Detroit’s starter misses a week, and skip the backups in Indianapolis entirely.

Projected effect: +10% to +16% above a handcuff-only price

Theme: target concentrationWRCHINov 23

Chicago Has No Alpha. It Has a Three‑Way Tie, and Luther Burden III Holds the Part of It That Repeats.

Rome Odunze, Luther Burden III and Colston Loveland project within 6.2 points of each other, and no Bear gets more than 25.6% of the team’s catches. Odunze leads the three only because he is projected to score a third of Chicago’s receiving touchdowns on fewer than a fifth of its catches. Take the touchdowns out and Burden is 11.6 points ahead.

Every group project has the same ending. The teacher asks who did the work, and three hands go up. One kid wrote the most pages. One kid made the slides. One kid stood up front and read the best line out loud, so that is the kid everybody remembers. Hold that group project. It comes back.

The site’s projections hand Chicago’s passing game to three people at once. Odunze projects to 211.1 points, Burden to 205.3 and Loveland to 204.9. That is 6.2 points from top to bottom, less than half a point a game. Now look at how much of the offense the top guy owns. In Seattle, Jaxon Smith‑Njigba gets 38.0% of the team’s projected catches. In Los Angeles, Puka Nacua gets 36.4%. Chicago’s leader is Loveland, a tight end, at 25.6%. That ranks 17th of 32 teams. This is not a target tree. It is a hedge.

So who is the WR1 in Chicago? The sheet says Odunze, by 5.8 points. The way he gets there is the problem. He projects to 59 catches, the fewest of the three, and 8.4 receiving touchdowns. That is 33.5% of the 25.1 receiving touchdowns Chicago projects to, on 18.9% of the catches. He is the kid who read the best line out loud. Burden projects to 75 catches and 937 yards, and Loveland to 80 catches. They wrote the pages.

The mechanism is what repeats. Catches come from being the guy the quarterback looks for on every down, and that carries from week to week. Touchdowns come from a handful of plays near the goal line, and they wander. Take them out and the order flips: Burden 172.3, Loveland 168.9, Odunze 160.7. In a split this flat, that is the share to own, because if any of the three breaks away from the pack, the one already catching the most balls is the one with the most to grow into. Odunze would need to pull more catches away from two teammates who already have more of them than he does.

Where this sits with the rest of the site. Our July 4 camp report said to buy Burden because DJ Moore and Olamide Zaccheaus left 166 targets behind, and the Play‑Caller Premium’s August 19 call named Burden as the one to absorb them, at a mid‑tier receiver price. We agree with both, and this table is the receipt. Our August 17 insight drop went the other way and called Odunze the clear inheritor of those targets. We disagree with that one, and the reason is printed above: the projection set every board here prices from gives Odunze 59 catches and gives Burden 75. If Odunze inherited the looks, the sheet would show it in his catches, and it does not. What it shows is a lot of his points coming from the end zone.

The risk, out loud: Odunze’s 17.4 yards a catch is real, and a deep receiver can keep a high touchdown rate longer than a slot receiver can. Burden strained his groin on August 8 and missed the rest of the preseason; our camp desk said it would not cost him Week 1, but a soft‑tissue injury can come back. And the Play‑Caller column warned that Ben Johnson’s run‑first offense makes weekly scores swing, so this is a better season bet than a sure thing every Sunday. Back to the group project. The teacher grades the kid who read the line out loud. Your league grades the pages.

The Pick: Luther Burden III (CHI, WR). Bid him at the same price as Odunze, not at the WR27 discount, and treat any touchdowns he adds as free upside on the biggest catch share of the Chicago wide receivers. Price Rome Odunze (CHI, WR) as the low end of the trio, around 161 points of catches and yards plus a touchdown count nobody can promise, and let someone else pay for “clear inheritor.”

Projected effect: +7% to +12% above his current price

Theme: positional scarcityTEARINov 22

Tight End Scarcity Is Two Players Deep. Pay for Trey McBride or Pay a Dollar, Because the Middle Is a Waiting Room.

Trey McBride and Brock Bowers each project to beat the 12th tight end by more than 71 points. The seven tight ends ranked TE5 to TE11 sit within 11.5 points of each other, less than a point a game. At tight end the scarce thing is two players, not the position.

Airlines board in groups. Group 1 is a few people with a fancy card. Then there is Group 5, which is everybody else, standing in a crowd by the gate, pretending to be Group 3. Nobody in Group 5 gets on the plane sooner by standing closer to the door. Hold that crowd at the gate. It comes back.

Tight end is the position fantasy players call “scarce,” and the site’s projections say that is half true. Measure each tight end against the 12th one, the starter a 12‑team league is left with when everyone has one, and the board splits in two. McBride projects to 241.1 points, 72.0 more than Mark Andrews at TE12. Bowers is 240.4, 71.3 more. Then there is a 30.4‑point drop to Tyler Warren at TE3. After Colston Loveland at TE4, the rest of the starters fall into one crowd: Harold Fannin Jr. at 188.5 down to Dallas Goedert at 177.0. Seven players, 11.5 points, 0.7 a game.

Now put those gaps next to the other positions, measured the same way against each one’s last starter: RB24 and WR36 in a 12‑team league. McBride’s 72.0 is about what Saquon Barkley at RB10 (75.8) and Rashee Rice at WR8 (75.6) give you over their own replacements. So McBride is not a luxury. He is a top‑ten running back who happens to line up next to the tackle. Kittle’s 18.7 is about what Carnell Tate at WR29 gives you, 18.4. Nobody pays a star price for WR29. People do it at tight end every year, because “TE7” sounds like a starter and “the 29th receiver” sounds like a bench.

The mechanism is catches. McBride projects to 108 of them and Bowers to 99. No other tight end reaches 86. A tight end’s points are mostly volume, and only two of them get receiver volume. Everyone from TE5 to TE11 is between 71 and 84 catches, which is why they score the same. Our July 30 insight drop argued that Arizona’s switch to Jacoby Brissett actually helps McBride, because a short passing game feeds the middle of the field, and the Play‑Caller Premium says the same about mismatch tight ends outlasting bad quarterbacks. We agree with both, and this table is why it matters: his floor is the thing the position does not have.

And the cheap end is not a punt. Our August 17 drop called Goedert a direct beneficiary of the A.J. Brown trade. He is already TE11, and he trails Kittle by 10.8 points, 0.6 a game. If the extra targets show up, he joins the crowd ahead of him for a fraction of the cost. Our camp desk’s September 8 report said to price Kittle as a healthy, full‑workload starter, and we are not arguing with that. A healthy Kittle is exactly what is on this sheet, and a healthy Kittle is still in the crowd.

The risk, out loud: touchdowns can close a gap that catches open. McBride projects to only 5.3 of them, and a tight end in the crowd who scores like Andrews does (7.1) would climb out of it. The top two are also the two most expensive tight ends on the board, so paying the full price for one eats money you would otherwise spend on a running back. That is the trade: a top‑ten running back’s edge, bought at tight end. And in a league that starts two tight ends, or pays a bonus for tight end catches, the crowd thins out and every rank in it costs more. Back to the gate. Either board with Group 1 or wait comfortably in your seat for Group 5. Standing by the door with a TE7 ticket gets you on the same plane, just poorer.

The Pick: Trey McBride (ARI, TE). Bid him like the top‑ten running back his 72.0‑point edge says he is, and do not let the word “tight end” scare you off the price. If you miss on him and Bowers, skip the TE5 to TE10 bidding war and take Dallas Goedert (PHI, TE) for a dollar or two at the end.

Projected effect: +8% to +14% above his current price
