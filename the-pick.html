The Pick

Market Desk·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on your lineup, your waiver claim and your draft board alike.

This week’s boardThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

Take the Touchdowns Away From the Top Five Backs and Bijan Robinson Is RB1 by 23 Points

Jahmyr Gibbs leads Bijan Robinson by 13.1 projected points, and 36.6 of his points come from touchdowns he has not scored yet. Strip the end zone out and Robinson leads every back in the pool.

A touchdown is the most generous thing in fantasy football and the least reliable. It pays six points for a play that may have been a coin flip, a goal‑line audible, or a guard who fell on a fumble. Nobody ever wrote a thank‑you note to the offensive line for it, and the offensive line never got an invoice for it either. That is the problem with a ranking built on it: you are paying a sure price for a lottery ticket.

So here is the test. Take the five highest backs in this site’s projections, full PPR over 17 games, and subtract every projected rushing and receiving touchdown at six points apiece. What is left is the part of the season that comes from carries, yards and catches. Gibbs is RB1 at 364.7 points, and 17.9 of his projected touchdowns are worth 107.4 of them. Take those out and he has 257.3. Robinson is RB2 at 351.6 with 11.8 touchdowns, worth 70.8. Take those out and he has 280.8, the best number of any back in the pool.

The gap is the whole story. Robinson trails Gibbs by 13.1 points with touchdowns and leads him by 23.5 without them. Gibbs has 6.1 more projected touchdowns than Robinson, and that, not a better set of legs, is what puts him first. Put it as a break‑even: if Gibbs scores 2.2 fewer touchdowns than the projection gives him, 15.7 instead of 17.9, the two are level. Running backs on strong offenses miss by that much all the time.

Now the mechanism. Robinson’s points come from the parts of the game that repeat. He projects 1,372 rushing yards and 76 catches for 708 more yards, and the catches alone are 76 points before a single yard is counted. Catches do not need the defense to break a tackle at the goal line. They need a quarterback who looks his way on third down, and that is a habit, not a mood. Christian McCaffrey, RB3 at 341.3, has the same shape: 79 catches, and 257.9 points without touchdowns.

The risk, out loud: this is a floor argument, not a prediction. Touchdowns are real points, and Gibbs may score every one of his. Detroit’s offense is good enough that 17.9 is not a fantasy. Also, regression cuts both ways, and Robinson’s 8.4 rushing touchdowns could just as easily run high. The claim is narrower: when two backs are 13.1 points apart and 36.6 of that comes from a number that swings hardest, the cheaper one is the better buy.

The line for your league: touchdowns are the tip, and you should not tip first.

The Pick: Bijan Robinson (ATL, RB). If the room prices Gibbs a full step above him, let Gibbs go and take Robinson, because he is RB1 on everything that repeats. Do not bid up Jahmyr Gibbs (DET, RB) past Robinson’s number: his lead is 2.2 touchdowns wide.

Projected effect: +5% to +9% above his current price

Theme: target concentrationWRSEAOct 27

Jaxon Smith‑Njigba Gets 44 of Every 100 Seattle Catches, and You Are Pricing Him Like He Shares

Among Seattle’s projected wide receivers and tight ends, Jaxon Smith‑Njigba owns 117 of 265 catches, a 44.2% share. Only Houston is higher, and Smith‑Njigba is the one of the top four who is a top‑three receiver on points.

Most offenses are a potluck. Everyone brings a side dish, nobody eats a full plate, and the quarterback spends the afternoon saying “great to see you” to people he threw to twice. A few offenses are a buffet with one guest who has already cleared the table. That guest is worth paying for, because your fantasy score is made of the plate, not the potluck.

Here is the measure. For every team in the projection set, add up the projected receptions of its wide receivers and tight ends, then see how much of that pile belongs to the top man. Houston is first at 44.4%, with Nico Collins at 87 of 196. Seattle is second at 44.2%, Cincinnati third at 44.1% (Ja’Marr Chase, 120 of 272) and the Rams fourth at 42.7% (Puka Nacua, 123 of 288). At the other end of the line, Buffalo spreads it so thin that its top receiver has 24.9%, Jacksonville is at 23.3%, and Miami is at 19.2%.

Share matters because it is the part of a receiver’s year that does not depend on the quarterback having a good week. When 44 percent of the catches go to one man, a bad game plan has to find a way around him. When 23 percent do, any Sunday can hand the ball to somebody else. Jacksonville projects six receivers between 42 and 70 catches. Seattle projects one at 117 and then AJ Barner at 45, Rashid Shaheed at 43 and Cooper Kupp at 38.

Now the part that makes it a pick. Share alone is not the whole story, because a big slice of a small pie is a snack. Collins owns the same slice as Smith‑Njigba but his pile is 196 catches, so he lands at 247.9 points, WR11. Smith‑Njigba’s slice sits on a 265‑catch offense and comes to 326.2, WR3, 33.1 points clear of Justin Jefferson at 293.3. Concentration and volume together: that is the combination worth a premium. The site has been on this one since its July 5 camp report, which said to pay up for him. Nothing here argues with that call; the numbers just say why.

The risk, out loud: a high share is a bet on one body. If Smith‑Njigba misses a month, Seattle’s other receivers do not add up to him, and you will have paid for a buffet that closed. Also, the share counts only the players in this pool, so it is a ruler, not a law. Chase and Nacua score more and carry nearly the same share, so if the room prices them at a discount to him, take them first.

The line for your league: the second‑best receiver on a team is a side dish, and you do not pay main‑course prices for side dishes.

The Pick: Jaxon Smith‑Njigba (SEA, WR). Count him as a top‑three receiver and bid like it, because 44 percent of his offense runs through him. Skip the potluck: Khalil Shakir (BUF, WR) projects 24.9% of Buffalo’s catches and is not worth a bid above his cheapest comparable.

Projected effect: +6% to +11% above his current price

Theme: tier cliffsRBCINOct 26

Ten Running Backs Fit Inside 21 Points, So Do Not Pay Chase Brown Like He Is Ten Spots Behind Jeanty

From RB6 to RB15 the projected running back board runs 279.6 down to 258.6, a 21.0‑point plateau. Chase Brown sits in the middle of it at 270.7, and the cheapest way to cover it is to take whichever of these backs the room forgets.

Every auction has a moment when two guys bid against each other for a running back who is worth exactly the same as the one nobody is looking at. It is a fine tradition. It is also how a roster ends up with a $48 back and a $31 back who score the same on Sunday.

Here is the board, in full PPR over 17 games. Jahmyr Gibbs is 364.7, Bijan Robinson is 351.6 and Christian McCaffrey is 341.3, an average of 352.5 for the top three. Then comes a real step down: Jonathan Taylor is 315.6, 25.7 behind McCaffrey, and De’Von Achane is 292.9. That is a cliff, and you pay for the ledge you land on.

Past Achane the board stops falling and starts lying flat. Ashton Jeanty is 279.6. James Cook is 278.3, Jeremiyah Love is 276.8, Derrick Henry is 273.2, Saquon Barkley and Kenneth Walker III are both 272.6, Breece Hall is 271.3, Chase Brown is 270.7, Omarion Hampton is 261.7 and Javonte Williams is 258.6. That is ten backs inside 21.0 points, with an average of 271.5. Over 17 games it is a bit over a point a week between the sixth back and the fifteenth.

Then the floor drops again. Travis Etienne is RB16 at 245.5, 13.1 behind Williams, and Kyren Williams is 231.1 after that. So the shape of the board is three stars, a short staircase, a ten‑man shelf, and a second cliff. A shelf is where you save money, because the market will charge a different price for each of ten players who project the same.

Why Brown out of ten? Because the site’s June 25 camp report already called him Cincinnati’s clear lead back, with Samaje Perine behind him, and a clear lead is the kind of role that holds a number steady. We have no reason to argue with that call. We just notice that the market tends to price the name on the shelf, not the shelf.

The risk, out loud: a shelf is a projection, and a projection is a median. Any of those ten can break in either direction, and the 21 points between them is smaller than one bad injury. If you cannot get a shelf back at a shelf price, the move is the step above: Taylor at 315.6 is 44.9 points over Brown.

The line for your league: when ten players share 21 points, the first one you nominate is the one you do not want.

The Pick: Chase Brown (CIN, RB). Treat him as one of ten equal backs and stop bidding when his price passes the shelf’s cheapest. If the room is paying star money for the shelf, pay up once for Jonathan Taylor (IND, RB) and let the rest go.

Projected effect: +6% to +12% above his current price
