The Pick

Nate Vega·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on your lineup, your waiver claim and your draft board alike.

This week’s boardThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

Theme: scoring settingsRBCINOct 4

Switch the Scoring From Standard to PPR and Derrick Henry’s 45‑Point Lead Over Chase Brown Shrinks to 2.5

Brown gets 23.6 percent of his 270.7 points from 64 catches. Henry gets 7.7 percent of his 273.2 from 21. In full PPR they are a tie, so stop paying a standard‑league price gap for them.

Every league has a rules page that nobody reads and everybody is governed by. It is the one document in fantasy football that moves money without anybody voting on it. One line, “1 point per reception,” sounds like a rounding error. It is not. It is a transfer of wealth from the men who carry the ball to the men who catch it, and it happens quietly, at the auction table, while your league argues about a kicker.

Here is the experiment. Take each back’s full PPR projection from this pool and subtract one point per catch to get the standard version. Now look at who moves. Derrick Henry projects for 1,484 rushing yards and 21 catches, so he is 252.2 points in standard and 273.2 in PPR: a 21‑point swing. Chase Brown projects for 1,038 rushing yards and 64 catches, so he is 206.7 in standard and 270.7 in PPR: a 64‑point swing. The 45.5‑point gap between them in standard becomes 2.5 in PPR.

Brown is not alone. Ashton Jeanty (65 catches) goes from 214.6 to 279.6, and Jeremiyah Love (65) from 211.8 to 276.8. Among the top 24 backs, the average is 48.3 catches, so a back at 64 or 65 is carrying about 16 more points of PPR value than the typical back, and a standard sheet never sees them.

Why Brown, and not the other two catch‑heavy backs? Because the role behind the catches is the one the site has already vouched for. Cincinnati’s camp reports had him around 69 to 70 percent of the backfield snaps with Samaje Perine well behind him, and his 435 projected receiving yards sit on top of the rushing line. That is a passing‑game role, and a role like that does not need a big touchdown total to pay off.

Now the mechanism. Many auction rooms carry a price memory from older, standard‑flavored rankings, where a 1,484‑yard bruiser outranks a 1,038‑yard back by a mile. If your league pays a point per catch, that gap is imaginary. Henry at RB9 money and Brown at RB13 money differ by 2.5 points over the whole season, which is about a seventh of a point a week.

The risk, out loud: this is an entry about your settings, and it flips if you play standard. In standard, Brown is the RB15 among the top 24 and Henry is the RB5, and everything above reverses. It also depends on the catches showing up. Sixty‑four is a projection, not a contract, and a Cincinnati offense that falls behind more or less than expected changes the number. Check the rules page before you bid.

The line to take to your league: a scoring setting is not a footnote, it is a price list. If a catch is worth a point, a running back who catches 64 passes has already been paid.

The Pick: Chase Brown (CIN, RB), and in a full PPR room the move is to treat him as a Henry‑sized asset: bid within a dollar or two of what Derrick Henry (BAL, RB) costs and let somebody else pay for the extra 446 rushing yards that PPR does not reward.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% above his current price

Theme: tier cliffsWRDALOct 3

The Receiver Board Falls Off Two Cliffs, Then Becomes a Parking Lot, and George Pickens Is Parked in the Cheap Spot

Receivers 1 through 4 are worth 335.5 points on average. Receivers 9 through 16 are separated by 11.5 points in total. Past the second cliff, a receiver is a spot in a crowd, so stop paying for the spot.

Picture a staircase in an old building. The first flight is real: you climb, it hurts, you arrive somewhere. Then comes a landing the size of a high school gym, and everyone standing on it is paying for a different floor. That is the shape of the receiver board in this projection set, and it matters because the auction prices every receiver like he is on his own step.

Line the top of the board up and read the gaps. Puka Nacua (356.0), Ja’Marr Chase (336.6), Jaxon Smith‑Njigba (326.2) and Amon‑Ra St. Brown (323.3) are the first tier. Then the floor drops 30.0 points to Justin Jefferson (293.3) and CeeDee Lamb (292.7), who are practically the same player on paper. Then it drops another 23.7 to Drake London (269.0), and a further 9.0 to Rashee Rice (260.0). That is three real steps in eight receivers.

After Rice the stairs end. Chris Olave (249.7), A.J. Brown (248.4), Nico Collins (247.9), Garrett Wilson (247.9), Malik Nabers (244.6), DeVonta Smith (238.8), George Pickens (238.6) and Zay Flowers (238.2) fill the next eight slots. From the first of them to the last is 11.5 points over a 17‑game season, about two thirds of a point a week. Their average is 244.3. If you ranked that group by a coin flip, your season would barely notice.

Here is the mechanism. An auction budget is a fixed pile of dollars, and a cliff is where a dollar buys the most. Paying up for a first‑tier receiver buys 30 points over the next step. Paying up for Olave over Pickens buys 11.1 points, and the next receiver down the board sits at 238.2, so there is no step below him to fall onto either. The market tends to smooth this out: it prices WR9 like WR9 and WR15 like WR15, as if the ranks were evenly spaced. They are not, and the unevenness is the whole edge.

So the plan is a barbell. Either pay for the cliff, or skip the whole parking lot until the cheap end of it. Pickens projects for 80 catches, 1,112 yards and 8.2 touchdowns, and he catches them in the same offense as Lamb. The site has already noted that Dallas added him next to Lamb and built one of the deepest receiver rooms in the league. That depth is a reason his number is not bigger, and it is already in his 238.6.

The risk, out loud: this is a case about price, not a case that Pickens is secretly a star. Eleven points is real but small, and any one receiver in the lot can lose a month to a hamstring. Touchdowns also do more of the work in his line than in Olave’s (49.2 points against 40.2), which is the wobbly part. If someone in your room is paying Olave money for Pickens, let them, and spend the savings on the step above.

The line to take to your league: a ranking is a list, but a price is a staircase, and most of the receiver board is a landing. Pay for the stairs. Rent on the landing.

The Pick: George Pickens (DAL, WR), and the move is to treat him as the cheap seat on the landing: bid as if he is the 16th receiver, not the 9th, and put the dollars you saved toward Drake London (ATL, WR), the last man on the board before the floor drops.

Projected effect: +5% to +9% above his current price

Theme: touchdown regressionWRNYJOct 2

Take the Touchdowns Away and Garrett Wilson Is the WR8, While Derrick Henry Drops From RB9 to RB15

Henry gets 31.4 percent of his 273.2 points from 14.3 touchdowns. Wilson gets 12.8 percent of his 247.9 from 5.3. Strip the scores out and the two men trade places on your board.

A touchdown is the fantasy equivalent of a restaurant tip: it feels like part of the bill, but it is the one piece the waiter does not fully control. Yards come from a role. Touchdowns come from a role plus a goal line plus a defensive coordinator who happened to be thinking about lunch. Every projection carries both, and the second kind is the one that likes to move around from year to year.

So here is the test. Take each player’s full PPR projection and subtract six points for every touchdown, rushing or receiving. What is left is the part of the line that comes from carries, catches and yards. Across the top 24 running backs in this projection set, touchdowns are 24.0 percent of the points, an average of 10.6 scores each. Across the top 24 receivers it is 18.1 percent, 7.8 scores each. Anyone far above those lines is leaning on the part of the stat sheet that regresses.

Start with the good news for the man in the headline. Wilson projects for 100 catches and 1,172 yards, and only 5.3 touchdowns. That is 216.1 points before a single score, the eighth‑best total of any receiver in the pool, ahead of Chris Olave (209.5) and A.J. Brown (206.4). With the touchdowns he is the WR12, tied with Collins at 247.9. The reason he sits lower is nothing he does between the 20s. It is that his touchdown total is modest, and modest is the number most likely to be a pleasant surprise.

Now the other side. Henry is projected for 1,484 rushing yards and 14.3 touchdowns, which is the most touchdown‑heavy line of any back in the top 12. Without the scores he is 187.4, which would rank 15th among backs, behind Chase Brown, Kenneth Walker, Saquon Barkley and James Cook. Javonte Williams has the same shape: 12.6 touchdowns, 29.2 percent of his points, and a drop from RB15 to RB16 once the scores are gone. Adams is the receiver version, with 10.6 touchdowns on 68 catches, more than a quarter of his line.

The site has not argued against Henry, and this entry does not either. The Play‑Caller column says the Ravens still run through Lamar Jackson’s legs and Henry’s carries and that he needs no install period, and a big role is the right reason to expect a big touchdown total. That is the honest case for him. The disagreement here is about price, not about role: a back whose rank depends on 14 scores should cost less than a back whose rank does not, and in most auctions he costs the same.

Say the risk out loud. Touchdowns are noisy, but they are not random. Backs on good offenses with goal‑line carries do score more, and Henry is exactly that, so his number could hold or go up. Wilson’s could just as easily stay low: the Jets are not a team that sends touchdowns to receivers by the dozen, and the camp desk reported he was a full participant in the offseason program after last year’s knee injuries, which means the whole case also needs him healthy. The argument is that Wilson’s line is built from the parts that repeat, not that he will outscore Henry.

The line to take to your league: a touchdown is a loan, and some projections are carrying more debt than others. Before you pay full price for a number, ask what is left of it when you subtract the part that regresses.

The Pick: Garrett Wilson (NYJ, WR), and the move is to price him as the WR8 he is without the touchdowns, not as the WR12 he is with them. On the other side, Derrick Henry (BAL, RB) is fine, but do not stretch for him: at RB9 money he is giving you RB15 production plus 14 scores you have to hope for.

Projected effect: +6% to +11% above his current price
