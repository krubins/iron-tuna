The Pick

Nate Vega·Market Intelligence Analyst·Iron Tuna

One story a day. Each one takes a single idea — scarcity, scoring settings, tier cliffs, the lies a backfield tells — runs the projection numbers until the idea either holds up or dies, and ends with the one player you should do something about. Read it for fun. Use it on your lineup, your waiver claim and your draft board alike.

This week’s boardThe Play-Caller Premium

How to read it. Every entry has a theme, a table, and a pick. The theme is the argument. The table is the evidence, pulled from the same projection set that prices every board on this site — so you can check it against your own sheet instead of taking our word for it. The pick is the part you act on.

Points are full PPR unless an entry says otherwise, over a 17‑game season. When scoring changes the answer, the entry says so out loud, because that is usually the whole story.

The percentage at the bottom of each entry is our estimate of how far the market price is off, not a projected stat line. The same edge is worth different money in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we translate it into your budget and your scoring right underneath.

Theme: positional scarcityTEARIOct 1

Trey McBride Is Worth as Much Over a Replacement Tight End as Drake London Is Over a Replacement Receiver, and Nobody Prices Him That Way

Measured against the player you would otherwise start, McBride is worth 72.0 points, about the same as the WR7 and the RB15. He is the TE1 in a position where only two men are worth that much.

“Positional scarcity” is a phrase people say in drafts the way they say “it is what it is” at funerals: it sounds wise and commits you to nothing. So here is the version with numbers. Scarcity is not who ranks first at a position. It is how much you lose when you do not get him and have to start the guy on your waiver wire instead.

To measure that, set a replacement level for a 12‑team league: the QB12, the RB30, the WR30 and the TE12 in this projection set. That is Trevor Lawrence (285.6), Rico Dowdle (185.4), Michael Pittman (196.5) and Mark Andrews (169.1). Subtract each from the players above him and the whole board re‑sorts by what a player actually gives you. Jahmyr Gibbs is first at 179.3. Puka Nacua is third at 159.5. And Trey McBride, a tight end who scores 241.1 points, lands 24th at 72.0.

Read the middle of that table. McBride at 72.0 is a hair behind London at 72.5, and London is the WR7, a first‑round name in every format. Javonte Williams, the RB15, is at 73.2. Three players, three positions, one size of edge, which is the point: McBride is a first‑round‑sized edge wearing a tight end’s price tag. The market sorts by raw points, and raw points punish a position that scores less. Replacement level is the correction, and it says the TE1 is a top‑25 asset on the whole board.

The reason is the shape of the position. After McBride and Bowers, the next man, Warren, is 30 points back at 210.0, and by TE12 the line is down at 169.1. Only 11 tight ends in the pool beat the replacement line at all, while 29 running backs do. When there are 29 chances to find a useful back, you can afford to miss on one. When there are 11 chances at tight end and two of them are special, you cannot. That is scarcity: not that the position is thin, but that the gap between your pick and your fallback is wide.

The site has already said why McBride is the safer of the two. The Play‑Caller column wrote that Arizona released Kyler Murray, handed the offense to Jacoby Brissett and still has a run‑leaning identity, and that McBride is the one player a bad quarterback cannot shrink. His projection fits: 108 catches and 1,023 yards, a line built almost entirely on volume rather than on touchdowns, which are only 5.3 of his 241.1 points. Bowers is a near twin at 71.3, but he needs 6.6 scores to get there.

Say the risk out loud. A replacement line is a choice, and a different one changes the answer. If your league starts two tight ends or a flex that takes them, McBride is worth more, not less. If you set the baseline at the TE8 instead (Kyle Pitts, 184.4), his edge shrinks to 56.7, still bigger than any quarterback except Josh Allen. And Arizona’s offense under Brissett could simply be bad, which caps every number in the building. The argument is that his role is the safest part of that offense, not that the offense is good.

The line to take to your league: the tight end who scores 241 points is not a tight end, he is a receiver who happens to be classified differently, and the one place you are allowed to pay a first‑round price for a position that scores less is the place where the replacement is 70 points worse.

The Pick: Trey McBride (ARI, TE), and the move is to pay for him the way you would pay for the WR7, not the way you would pay for a tight end. If he is gone, Brock Bowers (LV, TE) is the same deal with more touchdown risk. If both are gone, stop: Warren is 30 points behind them, and the rest of the position is a waiver wire with better PR.

Projected effect: +7% to +12% above his current price

Theme: target concentrationWRHOUSep 30

Nico Collins Is Getting 42.5 Percent of Houston’s Receiving Yards, and You Are Pricing Him Like a Man Who Has to Share

Collins is projected for 1,203 of the 2,831 receiving yards the Texans’ backs, wideouts and tight ends combine for. That is the biggest slice of any real receiving corps in the pool, and he is only the WR11.

Every office has one guy who does all the work and one guy who sends the “great job, team!” email. Fantasy football has the same org chart. On some offenses the targets are handed out like a group project, and on others one receiver does the whole thing and the rest of the room signs the cover page. The question for a draft is which kind of team you are buying a piece of.

Here is the number. Across the 33 teams that have skill players in this projection set, the typical team’s top receiver by yards takes 28.6 percent of the team’s combined receiving yards. Collins takes 42.5. Among receivers who are not backup-plan running backs on a team with no passing game, nobody in the pool is higher. Jaxon Smith-Njigba (SEA) is next at 41.8, then Ja’Marr Chase (CIN) at 39.5 and Puka Nacua (LAR) at 39.3. That is a short list, and three of its four names are the top three receivers by points.

Now the mechanism. Collins is not WR11 because his slice is small. It is WR11 because the pie is small: Houston’s room adds up to 2,831 yards, while Smith-Njigba’s adds up to 3,753 and Nacua’s to 4,047. Collins gets 87 catches and 1,203 yards, and the next-best receiver on the team, Tank Dell, is at 26 catches and 317 yards. The next-best anyone, Dalton Schultz at tight end, is at 552 yards. Collins has more than double the yards of the second man on his own team, which is a sentence you can say about very few players without laughing.

Why that is worth money is the boring, useful part. A projection with a big share behind it has fewer ways to break. When the target tree is one trunk and some twigs, a bad week from a twig does not touch the trunk. Houston also just lost the second branch. The site’s camp desk reported that Jayden Higgins tore his ACL in August and is out for the year, and the Play-Caller column has already written that the job of top target in Houston has not gone to anyone but Collins in any season of DeMeco Ryans’ tenure. The projection set agrees: Higgins is at zero, and the room behind Collins is Dell at 72.5 points and Xavier Hutchinson at 49.8. This is not a room built to take his targets away.

Say the risk out loud. Concentration cuts both ways. If a team has one real option, the defense knows it, and the one option has to stay healthy for the whole plan to work. There is no Plan B in this offense; the Plan B is Dalton Schultz. Collins is also projected for 6.7 touchdowns, which is 40.2 of his 247.9 points and the least repeatable piece of the line. And Houston’s smaller pie means his ceiling is capped: he is a WR11 who plays like a WR11, not a secret WR3. The argument is that his floor is unusually firm for the price, not that he is going to win you the league.

Put it in terms your league will repeat. Collins is the only receiver in the pool who does 42 percent of his team’s receiving, and he is being drafted like a guy who does a third. Paying for a share you can see is the safest kind of value in a projection set, because you do not need anything to go right for it to show up. You just need him to keep running routes.

The Pick: Nico Collins (HOU, WR), and the move is to treat him as a firm WR1 in a thin market, not a shared one. Take him at his current WR11 price without flinching, and if a rival in your league is deciding between him and a WR2 on a crowded offense, tell them the crowd is the discount that does not exist here.

Projected effect: +8% to +14% above his current price

Theme: tier cliffsRBINDSep 29

The Running Back Cliff Is Real, and Jonathan Taylor Is the Last Man Standing on the Right Side of It

Four backs project at 315 points or more. The fifth is 22.7 points behind the fourth, and the next eight are separated by less than nine points in total. Pay for the top four, then stop paying.

Every league has a friend who says he “believes in tiers,” and then drafts like a man who has never seen a staircase. He takes the fifth back at fourth-back money because the name looks right on the roster page. Tiers are supposed to tell you where to spend and where to sit on your hands. Here is where this year’s projections say the drop-off is, and it is not subtle.

Read the drops down the column. Gibbs to Robinson is 13.1 points. Robinson to McCaffrey is 10.3. McCaffrey to Taylor is 25.7. Then Taylor to Achane is 22.7, and after that the staircase turns into a parking lot: from Jeanty at RB6 (279.6) to Chase Brown at RB13 (270.7), eight running backs are packed into 8.9 points. That is about half a point a game across eight different roster spots. A back at the top of that pack and a back at the bottom of it are, for lineup purposes, the same guy in a different hat.

So the price of being wrong is lopsided. Skip Taylor and your best realistic landing spot is a back who projects 22.7 points lower, and the one after that is 36 lower. Skip a back in the plateau and the next one in the plateau costs you almost nothing. Taylor is the last back the market can’t replace with a shrug, which is the definition of the last thing worth paying up for. The gap between him and the thirteenth back is 44.9 points, or about 2.6 a game, every week, for a starter you never have to think about.

The rest of the site’s work lines up with this. The camp desk has already published that Achane, the first man below the cliff, is priced as if the coach who built his game had never left Miami. He did. If Achane is a fade at his price, then the four backs above the cliff are the only ones the projection set says are still cheap for what they do, and the fourth of them is the one the room will let slide.

Say the risk out loud: Taylor’s line leans on touchdowns. He is projected for 11.6 rushing scores, which is 69.6 of his 315.6 points, about 22 percent. Touchdowns are the least repeatable thing in the projection, and if the real number is closer to eight he gives back about 22 points and lands near Achane. That is the case for not paying like he is Gibbs. It is not the case for skipping him, because even that worst case still leaves him ahead of every back in the plateau.

The Pick: Jonathan Taylor (IND, RB), and the move is to treat him as the end of your running back spending, not the start of it. Get him if the price holds at RB4 money, and if you miss, do not chase Achane. Sit in the RB6 to RB13 pack and take whoever falls, because the projection says it does not matter which one.

Projected effect: +8% to +14% above his current price
