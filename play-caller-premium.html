The Play-Caller Premium

A coach is a price. Some staffs have made the same position outperform for thirty years; others quietly tax a position their roster is built around. This column names the pattern, then names the player it moves this season.

How to read it. Every entry has the same two halves: a coaching tendency with a track record long enough to be checkable, and the specific 2026 player that tendency lands on. A pattern with no named player is trivia; a named player with no pattern behind him is a hunch.

The percentages are this desk’s estimate of the gap versus current market price, not a projection of a stat line — and they are worth different dollars in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, each one is translated into your own budget and scoring underneath the entry.

The board
Pay the premiumWRNEAug 20

Mike Vrabel already turned A.J. Brown into a target monster once, on a worse offense than this one

The pattern is not subtle. From 2019 through 2022, Vrabel’s Titans were a run‑first, defense‑built team with Ryan Tannehill at quarterback, and Brown was still a two‑time Pro Bowler who posted 1,075 yards in 2020 and a career‑high 1,496 in 2022 — the WR1 of an offense that had no business generating a WR1 season by target volume alone. Vrabel does not need a pass‑heavy scheme to feed his best receiver; he needs one true X, and he throws to him on early downs as readily as third down.

Philadelphia proved the arrow points the other way too: traded away from Vrabel, Brown still produced, but it took Jalen Hurts’s MVP‑caliber 2024 to get him there. Now the two are back together in New England, and the supporting cast makes the fit even cleaner — Romeo Doubs raises the floor around him without threatening his target share, and Drake Maye is exactly the ascending young arm this staff has previously built around.

Who it moves: A.J. Brown (NE, WR). The market is still pricing him off a down Eagles finish; the coach who already made him a focal point once is not new information the room has fully absorbed.

Projected effect: +8% to +14% versus price

Take the discountRBPITAug 20

Mike McCarthy has not built a workhorse backfield in almost two decades, and Pittsburgh is about to prove it again

Thirteen years in Green Bay produced exactly one true bell‑cow season — Eddie Lacy’s 2013 — surrounded on both sides by committees: Ryan Grant and Brandon Jackson, then Ty Montgomery, then Aaron Jones splitting touches with Jamaal Williams even once Jones proved himself a top‑12 talent. Dallas told the same story from the other direction: Ezekiel Elliott arrived a bell‑cow before McCarthy, and by 2021–2022 Tony Pollard’s efficiency had forced McCarthy into a real timeshare anyway. The through‑line is a coach who is comfortable leaving touches on the table rather than feeding one back into the ground.

Pittsburgh signed Rico Dowdle for early‑down and goal‑line work and is running him alongside incumbent passing‑down back Jaylen Warren, with the staff already describing a genuine two‑man plan rather than a clear starter. That is the McCarthy pattern arriving on schedule.

Who it moves: Jaylen Warren (PIT, RB) and Rico Dowdle (PIT, RB). Neither is being priced as a true committee back yet; both should be, and the PPR‑relevant passing work keeps Warren the safer of the two discounts.

Projected effect: -8% to -14% versus a bell-cow price, for both backs

Pay the premiumQBCARAug 20

Dave Canales has a reclamation habit, and Bryce Young is the second quarterback he is rebuilding

Canales left Tampa Bay after the 2023 season having helped author Baker Mayfield’s career revival — a Pro Bowl year for a quarterback who had bounced through three organizations in the four seasons prior. That is not a one‑man outcome, but the timing lines up with the coach as much as the player. Carolina hired him in 2024 to do the same job for Bryce Young, and the season went through exactly the arc that pattern predicts: benched in the first month, reinstated by midseason, and closing the year playing clearly better football — enough that the Panthers never looked back at the position.

A full second training camp inside the same scheme, with the early‑season benching now a year behind him, is when a Canales quarterback is supposed to take the next step.

Who it moves: Bryce Young (CAR, QB). He is still priced like a former bust rather than a passer whose own coach has already rebuilt one career from worse.

Projected effect: +10% to +16% versus price

Pay the premiumQBJAXAug 20

Liam Coen’s one year calling plays produced the best statistical season of Baker Mayfield’s career — now he has Trevor Lawrence instead

Coen took over Tampa Bay’s offense in 2024, one year after Canales’s own revival year with Mayfield, and Mayfield got measurably better again — a career high in touchdown passes on an offense built around play‑action and quick, high‑percentage throws that turned an inconsistent veteran into one of the most efficient passers in the league. Coen left for the Jaguars’ head job in 2025 on the strength of that season.

Lawrence is a different kind of project than Mayfield — a former No. 1 pick with the physical talent never in question, whose efficiency has been the inconsistent part of his game. A scheme that manufactured easy completions for a journeyman is arguably a better fit for a talented quarterback who has mostly needed the offense around him to ask less of him on early downs, not more.

Who it moves: Trevor Lawrence (JAX, QB). The market remembers four up‑and‑down years under three different play‑callers; it has not yet priced in the coach who just did this exact job for someone else.

Projected effect: +8% to +13% versus price

Pay the premiumRBLVAug 19

The zone tree has been printing running backs for thirty years, and Klint Kubiak just brought it to Las Vegas

No coaching family has a longer paper trail than this one. Mike Shanahan’s Denver turned a sixth‑round pick into Terrell Davis, then did it again with Mike Anderson, then again with Olandis Gary and Reuben Droughns. Gary Kubiak took the same outside‑zone system to Houston and got a 1,600‑yard season out of undrafted Arian Foster. Kyle Shanahan has done it in Atlanta and San Francisco with backs the league did not want. The constant is not the runner. It is the scheme: wide zone with a cutback lane manufactures yards the back did not have to earn.

Klint Kubiak — Gary’s son, third generation of the same playbook — now calls it for the Raiders. The tree’s reputation cuts both ways, and honest analysis has to say so: this is also the family that invented the committee, and a scheme that makes any back productive is a scheme with no reason to protect one back’s workload. That is the risk you are underwriting.

Who it moves: Ashton Jeanty (LV, RB). The usual zone‑tree argument is that the back is replaceable. Jeanty is the inversion: a genuine first‑round talent inside the system, rather than a system creating a back out of nothing. You are buying the scheme’s efficiency and a player good enough to hold the job against it.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% versus price, with committee risk as the stated downside

Pay the premiumRBKCAug 19

Andy Reid’s lead back keeps finishing above his price, and this year that back is Kenneth Walker

Twenty‑five years, two franchises, one pattern: Duce Staley, Brian Westbrook, LeSean McCoy, Jamaal Charles, Kareem Hunt, Clyde Edwards‑Helaire as a rookie, Isiah Pacheco. Reid’s lead back returns a top‑12 season far more often than his summer cost implies, and the mechanism is not volume — Reid has never been a 300‑carry coach. It is leverage. An offense this dangerous through the air plays against light boxes and starts drives in plus territory, so the carries a Reid back does get are worth more than the same carries somewhere else.

The 2026 wrinkle makes it louder, not quieter. Kansas City is no longer the pure pass funnel it was: more quarterback rushing and more efficient early‑down running, with a modest cap on raw pass attempts. That is the exact profile in which this pattern has historically paid.

Who it moves: Kenneth Walker III (KC, RB). He was already one of the biggest team‑change winners on the board; the coach is the second half of that argument, and the market tends to price the change of address without pricing the play‑caller.

Projected effect: +10% to +15% versus price

Pay the premiumTECLEAug 19

Todd Monken feeds the tight end everywhere he goes, and Cleveland just hired him

Play‑callers have positional habits, and Monken’s is the most legible in football: the tight end is a primary read, not an outlet. At Georgia the tight end was the offense. In Baltimore he ran a 60% run‑action rate with top‑four multi‑back usage and finished first in explosive pass rate and air yards per attempt, and the tight end room ate inside all of it. Heavy personnel that still throws downfield is the rarest and most tight‑end‑friendly combination there is.

Cleveland imported the blueprint whole — Monken and Travis Switzer, from the same Baltimore operation. A scheme change resets the entire target hierarchy, and the market is slow to believe a target hierarchy that has not happened yet.

Who it moves: Harold Fannin Jr. (CLE, TE). A projection of roughly 78 catches and 816 yards is featured‑receiver treatment, not second‑wave tight‑end scraps. He is priced as the latter.

Projected effect: +12% to +18% versus price

Two-sidedTE / WRNYGAug 19

John Harbaugh and Matt Nagy will play heavy in New York, and personnel is a target tax

Personnel groupings are the least glamorous coaching signal and one of the most reliable, because they are arithmetic rather than opinion. Harbaugh’s teams have always lived in two‑tight‑end and two‑back sets; league scheme analysis expects less 11 personnel in New York under Harbaugh and Nagy. Fewer three‑receiver snaps means fewer routes to divide among wideouts, and tight ends and backs inherit route share they simply did not have before.

The roster broadcasts the intent rather than leaving it to be guessed at: the Giants added Isaiah Likely and fullback Patrick Ricard. Teams do not sign a fullback to run three‑receiver sets.

Who it moves: Up — Isaiah Likely (NYG, TE), who arrives into exactly the usage his new coaches prefer. Down — the secondary receivers — Darius Slayton and Darnell Mooney — who lose snaps rather than talent. Malik Nabers is mostly immune: alphas get their targets in any personnel group, which is precisely what makes the players behind them the ones the scheme taxes.

Projected effect: +8% to +12% for the tight end, -10% to -15% for the secondary receivers

Two-sidedWRSFAug 19

Some play-callers solve coverage with one address — and San Francisco’s receiver room is now built that way

The archetype is Scott Linehan. His WR1s were monsters — Calvin Johnson’s 1,964‑yard season, Dez Bryant’s three straight 1,200‑yard, 12‑touchdown years — and almost nobody else in those rooms was startable. That is not an accident of talent. It is a play‑caller who answers every coverage by throwing to the same man, and it produces the most reliably lopsided fantasy rooms in the sport.

You do not need Linehan on a sideline to draft the pattern; you need a room shaped like his. San Francisco’s is. After Mike Evans arrived, the 49ers became the most top‑heavy receiver corps in football, with George Kittle and Christian McCaffrey absorbing the middle of the field and a long drop to the next wideout.

Who it moves: Up — Mike Evans (SF, WR), the address. Down — everyone behind him — the secondary 49ers receivers are best‑ball dart throws, not managed‑redraft starters, and the weekly distribution is the reason.

Projected effect: +5% to +9% for the WR1, and treat the WR2 and WR3 as format-dependent rather than mispriced

Pay the premiumRBDETAug 19

Drew Petzing throws to his running backs, and Detroit handed him the best one in football

Receptions are the most price‑inefficient statistic a running back can produce. Carries are contested, weather‑dependent and game‑script‑dependent; targets to a back are called plays. A coordinator who calls them is handing you the least volatile fantasy points on the field, and drafters still pay for rushing yards.

Petzing arrives in Detroit from an Arizona scheme that fed backs heavily in the passing game. He inherits a declared bellcow: Dan Campbell called Jahmyr Gibbs the team’s every‑down back for 2026, David Montgomery was traded to Houston, and Isiah Pacheco is the backup rather than a committee partner.

Who it moves: Jahmyr Gibbs (DET, RB). Note the format sensitivity: this is a reception‑driven edge, so it is worth materially more in full PPR than in standard, and more again in leagues that pay a premium on running‑back catches.

Projected effect: +4% to +8% versus price in full PPR

Two-sidedWR / RBTBAug 19

The best evidence a coach matters is what happens after he leaves — Ben Johnson gives you both halves

Coaching claims are usually unfalsifiable, which is why this one is worth more than most: you can watch the same offense with and without the same man. Detroit’s rushing success rate fell after Ben Johnson left, and scheme‑driven efficiency losses compound rather than average out — worse down‑and‑distance means fewer goal‑line trips, and touchdowns are where a between‑the‑tackles back’s fantasy value lives.

Meanwhile the offense he took over in Tampa Bay is being rebuilt in his image: run‑first, play‑action heavy, with a concentrated target tree after Mike Evans left for San Francisco.

Who it moves: Up — Emeka Egbuka (TB, WR), the most logical player on the roster to absorb the vacated work — buy at a mid‑tier receiver price and budget for boom‑bust weeks, because a run‑first offense makes the weekly distribution wider. Down — David Montgomery (HOU, RB), whose fade is an efficiency argument rather than a workload one.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% for the receiver, -4% to -8% for the back
