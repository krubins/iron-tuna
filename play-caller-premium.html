The Play-Caller Premium

A coach is worth real money in your auction. Some staffs have made the same position pay off for thirty years. Others quietly hold back a position their roster is built around. Every entry here names the coaching habit, then names the 2026 player it moves.

Build My Free Auction SheetAuction insights

How to read it. Every entry has two halves. First, a coaching habit with a track record long enough to check. Second, the 2026 player that habit lands on. A pattern with no player attached is just trivia. A player with no pattern behind him is just a hunch.

The tag on each entry is the verdict. Underpriced means the coach makes that player worth more than the room is charging, so bid past the sheet. Overpriced means the coach makes him worth less, so let someone else pay. Two-sided means the same scheme lifts one player by taxing another, and the entry names both.

The percentages are our estimate of how far off the market price is. They are not a projected stat line. The same edge is also worth different dollars in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we convert each one into your budget and your scoring right under the entry.

The board
UnderpricedWRTBAug 23

Todd Bowles has kept a 1,000‑yard receiver in Tampa Bay every year he’s been the head coach — now it’s Emeka Egbuka’s turn

Since Bowles took over as Tampa Bay’s head coach in 2022, the Buccaneers have had a 1,000‑yard receiver every season, no matter who was calling plays or throwing the ball. Byron Leftwich ran the offense in 2022. Dave Canales took over in 2023. Liam Coen had it in 2024. Tom Brady retired and Baker Mayfield took over at quarterback in the middle of all that. The names around the job kept changing, and the job itself kept producing the same result anyway.

Mike Evans held that job through all of it, and now he is gone, traded to San Francisco. Egbuka steps into the opening as a second‑year receiver, and the numbers already treat him like the new No. 1: he projects for more catches, yards and touchdowns than any other Buccaneer, well ahead of a healthy Chris Godwin working from the slot.

Who it moves: Emeka Egbuka (TB, WR). The room is still pricing him like a No. 2 receiver finding his footing. Bowles’s track record says whoever holds that job in Tampa Bay gets fed like a true No. 1.

Projected effect: +7% to +12% above a No. 2 receiver price

UnderpricedRBLACAug 23

Mike McDaniel’s outside‑zone scheme keeps turning ordinary backs into monster rushers, and Omarion Hampton has no committee to share it with

In Miami, McDaniel’s wide‑zone rushing scheme produced huge, efficient seasons out of backs nobody expected it from. Raheem Mostert led the league in yards per carry in 2022 running behind it. Rookie De’Von Achane averaged close to eight yards a carry in 2023 in a part‑time role. The scheme creates the cutback lane. Whoever gets the ball turns modest gains into long ones.

McDaniel is now the Chargers’ offensive coordinator, and unlike in Miami, there is no timeshare cutting into the workload. Los Angeles did not draft a running back this offseason. Kimani Vidal is the only depth behind Hampton, and he projects for barely a third of Hampton’s rushing yards.

Who it moves: Omarion Hampton (LAC, RB). He is priced like a solid early‑down back in his second year. A zone scheme that has already made two different backs into league leaders in efficiency, with no real competition for his touches, is worth more than that.

Projected effect: +6% to +11% above his price, mostly from yards per carry

Two-sidedRBARIAug 23

Mike LaFleur has already shown he won’t hold a job open for an injured back, and Arizona’s backfield is about to test that again

LaFleur’s history with running backs is short but instructive. He was the Jets’ offensive coordinator for two seasons, and in the second one, rookie Breece Hall was off to an explosive start before tearing his ACL in Week 7 of 2022. The Jets did not build a committee to soften that loss and wait for Hall to come back. Whoever was healthy simply inherited the job for the rest of the season. LaFleur’s offense goes to the back in front of him, not the one on the roster with the pedigree.

Arizona spent the No. 3 overall pick and a record rookie contract on Jeremiyah Love, and the plan was always to hand him the offense. But Love suffered a high ankle sprain in the preseason opener, and LaFleur has not committed to a Week 1 timeline. James Conner, the healthy veteran directly behind him, is the back left standing.

Who it moves: Up — James Conner (ARI, RB), whose price still assumes he barely plays. LaFleur’s own history says the healthy back plays now, not later. Down — Jeremiyah Love (ARI, RB), still priced like a locked‑in Week 1 workhorse despite a coach who has already shown he won’t wait around when a back’s timeline is unclear.

Projected effect: +10% to +16% above a handcuff price for Conner in the short term; -8% to -12% off a locked-in workhorse price for Love until his timeline clears

UnderpricedQBTENAug 23

Brian Daboll turned Josh Allen into a deep‑ball weapon in Buffalo, and Cam Ward is his next reclamation project

Daboll called plays for Buffalo from 2018 through 2021, and Josh Allen’s deep passing improved every year he was there — more yards per attempt, more explosive completions, a genuine MVP‑level leap by 2020. Daboll did not change who Allen was. He changed how often the offense asked him to throw the ball down the field, and the numbers followed.

Ward’s rookie season did not go that way. He finished as a bottom‑half starter, averaging just 5.9 yards per attempt with only 15 touchdown passes, a profile built on short, safe throws instead of any downfield rhythm. Daboll’s arrival in Tennessee is a scheme change aimed at exactly that problem, and depth of target is the fastest way to fix a low yards‑per‑attempt number.

Who it moves: Cam Ward (TEN, QB). The market is still pricing him off a rookie season with almost no deep shots. Daboll has already fixed this exact issue for a different young quarterback once.

Projected effect: +8% to +12% above his rookie-year price

UnderpricedRBNOAug 22

Kellen Moore fed Ezekiel Elliott for four years, then turned Saquon Barkley into a 2,000‑yard back — now he calls the Saints’ plays

Moore called plays in Dallas from 2019 through 2022, and Elliott was a true bell‑cow through most of it, clearing 300 touches in three of those four seasons. Moore left for Philadelphia in 2024, installed the same zone‑heavy rushing structure, and Saquon Barkley ran for 2,005 yards — the second‑highest single‑season total in NFL history. Two different backs, two different rosters, the same outcome: whichever runner Moore hands the ball to gets fed like a lead back, not managed like a committee piece.

New Orleans is the next stop, and the backfield picture has already resolved itself in his favor. Alvin Kamara sprained his MCL in an August joint practice and is expected to miss at least a month, handing the early‑down and third‑down work to Travis Etienne Jr. Moore’s history says he does not hand a workload back in small pieces once a back has earned it, either.

Who it moves: Travis Etienne Jr. (NO, RB). The room is still pricing him for the committee that resumes the day Kamara is healthy. Moore’s track record says the back who takes the job in Kamara’s absence keeps more of it than that price assumes.

Projected effect: +8% to +13% above his committee price

UnderpricedQBINDAug 22

Shane Steichen’s offense manufactures quarterback rushing touchdowns, and Daniel Jones is the newest one to get them

Steichen called plays for Jalen Hurts in Philadelphia in 2021 and 2022, building the league’s most productive quarterback run game around him. Hurts scored 13 rushing touchdowns in that 2022 MVP‑runner‑up season, most of them on designed runs and short‑yardage keepers rather than scrambles. Steichen brought that same identity to Indianapolis as head coach in 2023, and it survived the switch from Anthony Richardson’s legs to Daniel Jones’s.

Jones was never miscast as a pocket passer in New York, but the Giants stopped using his legs as anything more than an escape valve. Steichen’s offense does not have that option. Designed quarterback runs and goal‑line keepers are a feature of the system, not an emergency plan, and the system does not care who is taking the snap.

Who it moves: Daniel Jones (IND, QB). The market is still pricing him off his passing line, the same way it always has. Steichen’s system adds rushing touchdowns to that price for free.

Projected effect: +9% to +14% above a passing‑only price

UnderpricedRBNYJAug 22

Defensive head coaches play it safe on offense, and Aaron Glenn’s Jets already look the part

A coach’s background is one of the most reliable shortcuts in football. Coaches who came up on defense tend to build offenses that protect field position and shorten the game — more called runs on early downs, more clock burned, fewer total possessions. Bill Belichick’s Patriots and John Harbaugh’s Ravens both ran this identity for over a decade, leaning on the running back long before either team had a great one at the position.

Glenn, a career defensive coach hired to run the Jets in 2025, fits the mold, and the 2026 numbers already show it before a snap is played: Geno Smith’s projected passing volume sits near the bottom of the league among full‑time starters, exactly what a program built to protect its defense produces.

Who it moves: Breece Hall (NYJ, RB). He is priced like a complementary back in an offense that still throws plenty. A staff built to shorten games leans on its lead back more than the raw pass‑attempt numbers suggest.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% above his price

UnderpricedRBDETAug 22

Dan Campbell never stopped daring it on fourth down, and that always creates a market for a short‑yardage back

No coach in the league goes for it on fourth down more often than Dan Campbell, and that is not a personality quirk — it is dozens of extra offensive snaps a season inside plus territory that a cautious coach would have punted or kicked away instead. Those plays cluster in exactly the situations where a physical, downhill runner earns his keep: third‑and‑short, fourth‑and‑one, the goal line. Detroit has leaned on a designated short‑yardage piece for that work rather than asking its lead back to do all of it, which is part of why the Lions’ rushing touchdowns have never belonged to one player the way the yardage does.

Isiah Pacheco arrives from Kansas City into exactly that kind of role, behind Jahmyr Gibbs rather than ahead of him. He does not need three‑down volume to matter in this offense. He needs the ball inside the five, and Campbell’s fourth‑down habit means he will see more of those snaps than a typical backup would.

Who it moves: Isiah Pacheco (DET, RB). He is priced as pure injury insurance. Campbell’s fourth‑down aggressiveness turns him into a real source of rushing touchdowns even with the starter healthy.

Projected effect: +10% to +18% above a handcuff‑only price, most of it in touchdowns rather than yards

UnderpricedWRNEAug 20

Mike Vrabel already turned A.J. Brown into a target monster once, on a worse offense than this one

The pattern is not subtle. From 2019 through 2022, Vrabel’s Titans were a run‑first, defense‑built team with Ryan Tannehill at quarterback. Brown still made two Pro Bowls there. He put up 1,075 yards in 2020 and a career‑high 1,496 in 2022 — the No. 1 fantasy receiver on an offense that did not throw enough to produce one. Vrabel does not need a pass‑heavy scheme to feed his best receiver. He needs one true No. 1, and he throws to him on early downs as often as on third down.

Philadelphia showed the other side of it. Traded away from Vrabel, Brown still produced, but it took Jalen Hurts’s MVP‑caliber 2024 to get him there. Now the two are back together in New England, and the rest of the offense fits even better. Romeo Doubs helps around him without taking his targets, and Drake Maye is exactly the kind of rising young quarterback this staff has built around before.

Who it moves: A.J. Brown (NE, WR). The room is still pricing him off a down finish in Philadelphia. It has not priced in the coach who already made him the focal point once.

Projected effect: +8% to +14% above his current price

OverpricedRBPITAug 20

Mike McCarthy has not built a workhorse backfield in almost two decades, and Pittsburgh is about to prove it again

Thirteen years in Green Bay produced exactly one true workhorse season: Eddie Lacy in 2013. On either side of it were committees — Ryan Grant and Brandon Jackson, then Ty Montgomery, then Aaron Jones splitting touches with Jamaal Williams even after Jones proved he was a top‑12 back. Dallas told the same story from the other end. Ezekiel Elliott was already a workhorse when McCarthy got there, and by 2021 and 2022 Tony Pollard had played well enough to force a real timeshare anyway. The thread is a coach who would rather leave touches on the table than run one back into the ground.

Pittsburgh signed Rico Dowdle for early‑down and goal‑line work, and is running him next to passing‑down back Jaylen Warren. The staff is already describing a real two‑man plan instead of a clear starter. That is the McCarthy pattern showing up right on schedule.

Who it moves: Jaylen Warren (PIT, RB) and Rico Dowdle (PIT, RB). Neither is priced like a committee back yet, and both should be. Warren catches passes, which makes him the safer of the two discounts in PPR.

Projected effect: -8% to -14% below a workhorse price, for both backs

UnderpricedQBCARAug 20

Dave Canales has rebuilt one quarterback’s career already, and Bryce Young is the second

Canales left Tampa Bay after the 2023 season, having helped turn Baker Mayfield’s career around. That was a Pro Bowl year for a quarterback who had bounced through three teams in the four seasons before it. No coach deserves all the credit, but the timing points at him as much as at the player. Carolina hired Canales in 2024 to do the same job for Bryce Young, and the season followed the arc that pattern predicts: benched in the first month, back in the lineup by midseason, and clearly playing better by the end. The Panthers have not looked at the position since.

A full second camp in the same system, with that benching a year behind him, is exactly when a Canales quarterback is supposed to take the next step.

Who it moves: Bryce Young (CAR, QB). He is still priced like a former bust, not like a passer whose own coach has already rebuilt a career from worse.

Projected effect: +10% to +16% above his price

UnderpricedQBJAXAug 20

Liam Coen’s one year calling plays gave Baker Mayfield the best season of his career — now he has Trevor Lawrence

Coen took over Tampa Bay’s offense in 2024, a year after Canales’s revival season with Mayfield, and Mayfield got better again. He set a career high in touchdown passes in an offense built on play‑action and quick, high‑percentage throws. That scheme turned an up‑and‑down veteran into one of the most efficient passers in the league, and it got Coen the Jaguars head job in 2025.

Lawrence is a different project. He was the No. 1 overall pick, and his physical talent has never been the question. His efficiency has. A scheme that manufactured easy completions for a journeyman may fit even better on a talented passer who mainly needs the offense around him to ask less of him on early downs, not more.

Who it moves: Trevor Lawrence (JAX, QB). The market remembers four up‑and‑down years under three different play‑callers. It has not yet priced in the coach who just did this exact job for someone else.

Projected effect: +8% to +13% above his price

UnderpricedRBLVAug 19

The zone‑scheme coaching tree has been making running backs for thirty years, and Klint Kubiak just brought it to Las Vegas

No coaching family has a longer paper trail than this one. Mike Shanahan’s Denver turned a sixth‑round pick into Terrell Davis, then did it again with Mike Anderson, then again with Olandis Gary and Reuben Droughns. Gary Kubiak took the same outside‑zone system to Houston and got a 1,600‑yard season out of undrafted Arian Foster. Kyle Shanahan has done it in Atlanta and San Francisco with backs the league did not want. The runner is not what stays the same. The scheme is. Wide zone with a cutback lane creates yards the back did not have to earn.

Klint Kubiak — Gary’s son, and the third generation running this playbook — now calls it for the Raiders. The family reputation cuts both ways, and it is only fair to say so. This is also the tree that invented the committee. A scheme that makes any back productive has no reason to protect one back’s workload. That is the risk you are taking on.

Who it moves: Ashton Jeanty (LV, RB). The usual knock on a back in this scheme is that anyone could do the job. Jeanty flips that: a real first‑round talent inside the system, instead of a system inventing a back out of nothing. You get the scheme’s efficiency and a player good enough to keep the job.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% above his price, with committee risk as the stated downside

UnderpricedRBKCAug 19

Andy Reid’s lead back keeps finishing better than his price, and this year that back is Kenneth Walker

Twenty‑five years, two teams, one pattern: Duce Staley, Brian Westbrook, LeSean McCoy, Jamaal Charles, Kareem Hunt, Clyde Edwards‑Helaire as a rookie, Isiah Pacheco. Reid’s lead back finishes in the top 12 far more often than his summer price suggests. Volume is not the reason — Reid has never been a 300‑carry coach. Field position and light boxes are. An offense this dangerous through the air faces fewer defenders in the box and starts drives closer to the end zone, so the carries a Reid back does get are worth more than the same carries somewhere else.

The 2026 change makes the pattern stronger, not weaker. Kansas City is no longer the pure passing offense it was. There is more quarterback running, more efficient early‑down running, and a small cap on raw pass attempts. That is the exact setup in which this pattern has paid before.

Who it moves: Kenneth Walker III (KC, RB). He was already one of the biggest winners of any team change on the board. The coach is the second half of that argument, and the room usually pays for the new team without paying for the play‑caller.

Projected effect: +10% to +15% above his price

UnderpricedTECLEAug 19

Todd Monken feeds the tight end everywhere he goes, and Cleveland just hired him

Play‑callers have position habits, and Monken’s is the easiest to read in football: the tight end is a first option, not a checkdown. At Georgia the tight end was the offense. In Baltimore he ran a 60% run‑action rate, used two‑back sets as often as almost anyone, and still finished first in explosive pass rate and air yards per attempt. The tight ends ate through all of it. An offense that lines up heavy and still throws deep is the rarest and friendliest setup a tight end can get.

Cleveland brought in the whole blueprint — Monken and Travis Switzer, from that same Baltimore staff. A scheme change resets who gets the targets, and the market is slow to believe a pecking order it has not seen yet.

Who it moves: Harold Fannin Jr. (CLE, TE). A projection of about 78 catches and 816 yards is No. 1 receiver treatment, not leftover tight‑end scraps. He is priced like the scraps.

Projected effect: +12% to +18% above his price

Two-sidedTE / WRNYGAug 19

John Harbaugh and Matt Nagy will play heavy in New York, and that costs the wide receivers targets

Which players a coach puts on the field is the least exciting signal there is, and one of the most reliable, because it is arithmetic rather than opinion. Harbaugh’s teams have always lived in two‑tight‑end and two‑back sets, and league scheme analysis expects fewer three‑receiver sets in New York under Harbaugh and Nagy. Fewer three‑receiver snaps means fewer routes to split among wideouts. The tight ends and backs pick up the routes they lose.

The roster says the plan out loud instead of leaving it to be guessed at: the Giants added Isaiah Likely and fullback Patrick Ricard. Teams do not sign a fullback to run three‑receiver sets.

Who it moves: Up — Isaiah Likely (NYG, TE), who lands in exactly the role his new coaches like to use. Down — the second‑tier receivers, Darius Slayton and Darnell Mooney, who lose snaps rather than talent. Malik Nabers is mostly safe: a true No. 1 gets his targets no matter who else is on the field, which is why the players behind him take the hit.

Projected effect: +8% to +12% for the tight end, -10% to -15% for the second-tier receivers

Two-sidedWRSFAug 19

Some play-callers answer every coverage by throwing to the same man — and San Francisco is built that way now

The classic example is Scott Linehan. His top receivers were monsters — Calvin Johnson’s 1,964‑yard season, Dez Bryant’s three straight years of 1,200 yards and 12 touchdowns — and almost nobody else in those rooms was worth starting. That was not luck with talent. It is what happens when a play‑caller answers every coverage by throwing to the same man, and it makes the most lopsided receiver rooms in the sport.

You do not need Linehan on the sideline to draft the pattern. You need a room shaped like his, and San Francisco has one. After Mike Evans arrived, the 49ers became the most top‑heavy receiver group in football, with George Kittle and Christian McCaffrey taking the middle of the field and a long drop to the next wideout.

Who it moves: Up — Mike Evans (SF, WR), the man they throw to. Down — everyone behind him. The other 49ers receivers are best‑ball dart throws, not weekly starters, and how uneven the targets are week to week is the reason.

Projected effect: +5% to +9% for the No. 1 receiver; treat the No. 2 and No. 3 as format calls, not bargains

UnderpricedRBDETAug 19

Drew Petzing throws to his running backs, and Detroit handed him the best one in football

Catches are the most underpriced thing a running back can do. Carries depend on the score, the weather and the defense in front of him. Targets to a back are called plays. A coordinator who calls them is handing you the steadiest fantasy points on the field, and drafters still pay for rushing yards instead.

Petzing comes to Detroit from an Arizona offense that threw to its backs often. He inherits a declared workhorse: Dan Campbell called Jahmyr Gibbs the team’s every‑down back for 2026, David Montgomery was traded to Houston, and Isiah Pacheco is a backup rather than a committee partner.

Who it moves: Jahmyr Gibbs (DET, RB). Watch your format here. This edge comes from catches, so it is worth much more in full PPR than in standard, and more again in leagues that pay a bonus on running‑back catches.

Projected effect: +4% to +8% above his price in full PPR

Two-sidedWR / RBCHIAug 19

The best proof a coach matters is what happens after he leaves — Ben Johnson shows both halves

Most claims about coaching cannot be proved either way, which is why this one is worth more than most: you can watch the same offense with him and without him. Detroit’s rushing success rate fell after Ben Johnson left, and losses like that pile up instead of evening out. Worse down and distance means fewer trips to the goal line, and touchdowns are where an inside runner’s fantasy value lives.

Meanwhile, the offense he took over in Chicago is being rebuilt in his image: run‑first, heavy on play‑action, and with the targets going to fewer players now that DJ Moore and Olamide Zaccheaus are both gone. That pair walked out with 166 targets between them, and they had spent most of 2025 lined up ahead of the receiver who now inherits them.

Who it moves: Up — Luther Burden III (CHI, WR), the most logical player to absorb those vacated targets after a rookie year that put him top five among receivers in catch rate (77%), yards per target (10.8) and yards per route run (2.9). Buy him at a mid‑tier receiver price and plan for boom‑or‑bust weeks, because a run‑first offense makes the week‑to‑week swings wider. Down — David Montgomery (HOU, RB), who fades because of efficiency, not because he loses carries.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% for the receiver, -4% to -8% for the back
