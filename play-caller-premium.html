The Play-Caller Premium

A coach is worth real money in your auction. Some staffs have made the same position pay off for thirty years. Others quietly hold back a position their roster is built around. Every entry here names the coaching habit, then names the 2026 player it moves.

Build My Free Auction SheetAuction insights

How to read it. Every entry has two halves. First, a coaching habit with a track record long enough to check. Second, the 2026 player that habit lands on. A pattern with no player attached is just trivia. A player with no pattern behind him is just a hunch.

The tag on each entry is the verdict. Underpriced means the coach makes that player worth more than the room is charging, so bid past the sheet. Overpriced means the coach makes him worth less, so let someone else pay. Two-sided means the same scheme lifts one player by taxing another, and the entry names both.

The percentages are our estimate of how far off the market price is. They are not a projected stat line. The same edge is also worth different dollars in different leagues, so if you have set your league up in the app, we convert each one into your budget and your scoring right under the entry.

Two-sidedRBLARSep 1

Sean McVay has never let one running back keep the job for long, and the pattern is back with Kyren Williams and Blake Corum

McVay does this every few years. Todd Gurley carried the offense in 2017 and 2018, but the moment his role started to erode in 2019, McVay did not hand the job to a single replacement — he opened 2020 by naming a three-man rotation of Malcolm Brown, Darrell Henderson and rookie Cam Akers instead. The committee kept reshuffling for three more seasons, with Henderson, Akers and Sony Michel all taking turns as the lead back, before Kyren Williams finally stabilized the job in 2023.

The pattern is back. Blake Corum is lined up for close to a 50‑50 split of the carries with Kyren Williams this season, continuing a share he already took down the stretch a year ago, per multiple outlets including Yahoo Sports and Sports Illustrated. Williams still opens the year as the starter and keeps the goal‑line work, so his job is not in danger — but McVay has never let a starter keep the whole backfield for long, and a real committee is a real committee, whichever name sits atop the depth chart.

Who it moves: Up — Blake Corum (LAR, RB), who profiles as a genuine committee partner now, not a cheap handcuff. Down — Kyren Williams (LAR, RB), still priced by some rooms like a true workhorse when his own backfield already expects to split it close to evenly.

Projected effect: +8% to +12% above a handcuff price for Corum; -6% to -10% below a workhorse price for Williams

UnderpricedWRGBSep 1

Davante Adams once had the whole Green Bay passing game to himself, and Christian Watson just inherited the same empty room

Adams’ last two seasons in Green Bay are the clearest proof of what happens when a Packers receiver has no real competition left in the room. He led the NFL with 18 receiving touchdowns in 2020, then followed it with a career‑high 123 catches and 1,553 yards in 2021, because there was nobody else on that roster a defense had to respect.

Green Bay has cleared the same kind of space around Christian Watson. The team signed him to a four‑year, $110.5 million extension this offseason, and with Romeo Doubs and Dontayvion Wicks both off the roster, camp reports already have him drawing the early targets as the clear No.‑1 — a 14‑yard catch on the second offensive snap of the preseason opener, and a target share that keeps climbing. His projection still lists him for just 55 catches, a number built before that competition left the building.

Who it moves: Christian Watson (GB, WR). He is priced like the boom‑or‑bust field‑stretcher he used to be in a crowded room. The room is not crowded anymore.

Projected effect: +8% to +13% above his current price, on target-share growth the projection has not caught up to yet

UnderpricedRBWASSep 1

An undrafted rookie can still take over an NFL backfield in one camp, and Jacory Croskey-Merritt is proving it in Washington the same way James Robinson once did in Jacksonville

James Robinson went undrafted out of Illinois State in 2020. Jacksonville released Leonard Fournette, a former top‑five pick, right before the season and turned the backfield over to Robinson instead of paying up for a proven veteran. He rushed for 1,070 yards as a rookie. A strong training camp has always counted for more than draft pedigree once a coaching staff actually has to pick someone.

Washington is watching the same story play out. Croskey‑Merritt went in the seventh round last year and still rushed for 805 yards and eight touchdowns on 175 carries as a rookie. Camp reporting already has him as the favorite to open the season atop the depth chart, with veteran Rachaad White — still working back from a hamstring injury — ticketed for a complementary role instead.

Who it moves: Jacory Croskey-Merritt (WAS, RB). He is still priced like a seventh‑round afterthought. The depth chart already reads like a lead back’s.

Projected effect: +9% to +14% above a late-round price, on the lead-back role camp has already handed him

OverpricedWRBUFSep 1

The Marv Levy‑era Bills never let one pass-catcher run away with a season, and this year’s room is doing it to DJ Moore

Buffalo’s no‑huddle offense under Marv Levy was built to spread the ball around, not feed one target. Thurman Thomas led the NFL in yards from scrimmage four straight seasons from 1989 through 1992 by adding fifty‑plus catches a year out of the backfield — on an offense that also started Andre Reed and James Lofton at receiver. Nobody in that group ever had the passing game to himself.

Buffalo’s current room is doing the same thing to DJ Moore. He arrived this offseason as the presumed No.‑1, but Dalton Kincaid and Dawson Knox have both stayed heavily involved, and the room is deep enough that Josh Allen’s best weeks can now run through any of three different pass‑catchers instead of one. That is good for Buffalo’s offense and bad for anyone pricing Moore like the passing game’s one focal point.

Who it moves: DJ Moore (BUF, WR). He is priced like a true alpha receiver. The room around him was built to make sure nobody gets to be one.

Projected effect: -6% to -10% below a true-No.1 price, on target diffusion across Kincaid and Knox

OverpricedTEATLAug 31

Kevin Stefanski has never let one tight end run away with the job, and he just brought his own second option to Atlanta

Stefanski ran a two-tight-end room for two full seasons as Cleveland’s head coach, pairing David Njoku with Austin Hooper from 2020 through 2021. Neither one topped 50 catches in either year. The Browns leaned on Nick Chubb and Kareem Hunt on the ground instead, and whatever passing-game work was left got split down the middle rather than funneled to a single featured tight end.

Atlanta just handed Stefanski the same setup. He signed Hooper as a free agent this offseason, and the preseason snaps are already following the old pattern: Kyle Pitts played only 9 of the Falcons’ 14 first-team snaps in the preseason opener, down from the 87.7% snap share he carried all of last season. Pitts is still the better player by a wide margin, but Stefanski’s own history says that doesn’t stop him from carving out a real role for the second tight end anyway.

Who it moves: Kyle Pitts (ATL, TE). He is still priced like a true top‑five target earner at the position. Stefanski has already shown once, with a similarly talented tight end room, that he won’t let the touches concentrate that way.

Projected effect: -8% to -12% below a 90-plus-target TE1 price, on snap and target-share dilution from the two-tight-end personnel

UnderpricedWRBALAug 31

An uncontested No. 1 receiver has put up monster years in far worse passing games than Baltimore’s, and Zay Flowers has zero real competition for the job

DeAndre Hopkins is the cleanest proof of how little a receiver needs around him if nobody else on the roster can take his targets. In 2015, playing for a run‑first Houston staff and catching passes from Brian Hoyer, a quarterback so unremarkable the team benched him during the season, Hopkins still caught 111 passes for 1,521 yards and 11 touchdowns. A shaky offense caps a receiver’s efficiency. It does almost nothing to a receiver nobody else on the roster can replace as the No. 1 read.

Baltimore is built the same way. The offense still runs through Lamar Jackson’s legs and Derrick Henry’s carries first, but Flowers is the only Ravens receiver anyone treats as a real threat — the projections have him at 82 catches and 1,171 yards, more than double Rashod Bateman’s 38 catches and 614 yards a tier behind him. That gap is what an uncontested No. 1 job looks like on paper before a single snap is played.

Who it moves: Zay Flowers (BAL, WR). He is priced like a complementary piece in a run-first passing game. The size of his lead over the next Ravens receiver says he is the exception a run-first offense still has to feed.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% above a run-first-offense discount price, on target-share concentration

Two-sidedRBMINAug 31

Kevin Faulk built a decade-long career in New England on third downs alone, and Minnesota’s backfield is already splitting the same way

Faulk never won a starting job in thirteen seasons with the Patriots and never had a 1,000‑yard season, but Bill Belichick kept him on the field for third downs and two‑minute drills year after year regardless of who else was in the backfield. A back who is safe in protection and reliable catching the ball out of the backfield is the hardest role to take away once a staff trusts him with it, even when he never touches the early‑down carries at all.

Minnesota’s Aaron Jones and Jordan Mason committee is already sorting itself along those same lines. The two have been described as a muddled timeshare all camp, but the receiving work is not split evenly: Jones projects for 47 catches this season against just 13 for Mason. The rushing competition is still unresolved. The passing-down job already looks decided.

Who it moves: Up — Aaron Jones (MIN, RB), whose receiving role gives him the safer weekly floor even if Mason wins more of the early‑down carries. Down — Jordan Mason (MIN, RB), priced like a clean lead back in a committee that already looks likely to cost him the passing downs.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% above a committee price for Jones, on receiving-role safety; -5% to -9% below an early-down-lead price for Mason, on the passing downs he has already lost

UnderpricedTEARIAug 30

A true mismatch tight end has always outrun a bad quarterback situation, and Trey McBride is proving it in the worst one in the league

Antonio Gates spent a decade and a half as the clearest example football has of this: whoever started at quarterback in San Diego, healthy or hurt, Gates stayed the first read in the red zone and on third down, because a tight end built to beat a linebacker or a safety one‑on‑one does not need a great arm behind him to stay open. A shaky quarterback shrinks the throwing windows every ordinary receiver on the field depends on. It barely touches a mismatch nobody on the field can actually cover.

Arizona is running the same experiment now, just from a worse starting point. The team released Kyler Murray and handed the offense to Jacoby Brissett, a downgrade that drags down the ceiling on every skill player in the building, on a roster already built around a run‑leaning identity rather than a featured passing game. McBride is the one player that downgrade does not really touch. His catch radius makes him the safety‑valve target no matter who is throwing him the ball, which is exactly the profile that has survived a bad quarterback before.

Who it moves: Trey McBride (ARI, TE). The market has already priced in Arizona’s quarterback downgrade for the receivers around him. It should not be pricing McBride the same way — his role is the one part of this offense a bad quarterback cannot shrink.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% above a standard TE1 price, insulated from Arizona’s other downgrades

Two-sidedRBDENAug 30

The receiving back is historically the safest job in a messy committee, and Denver’s crowded backfield is about to prove it again

Darren Sproles never won a single training‑camp competition for the nominal starting job — not in San Diego, not in New Orleans, not in Philadelphia. It never mattered. Across three franchises and three completely different coaching staffs over more than a decade, Sproles kept his third‑down and two‑minute role fully intact, because a back who is safe in pass protection and dangerous with the ball in space is the hardest role to take away once a staff trusts him with it. The name atop the depth chart changes constantly. The safest job in the room almost never does.

Denver is running exactly that kind of muddled committee right now. J.K. Dobbins is back on a two‑year deal and still projects for the bigger rushing workload on paper, but he has missed 54 games in six NFL seasons, and the touch split has stayed murky enough all camp that beat coverage keeps hedging on how the three‑man group divides up. RJ Harvey already took over as Denver’s lead back for stretches last season once Dobbins went down, and it is specifically his receiving role that gives him the safer weekly floor of the two, not his standing on the depth chart.

Who it moves: Up — RJ Harvey (DEN, RB), priced like the back half of an even timeshare when his receiving role alone gives him the safer weekly floor. Down — J.K. Dobbins (DEN, RB), priced like a clean lead back despite an injury history that has already cost him 54 games in six seasons.

Projected effect: +8% to +12% above a committee price for Harvey, on the safety of his receiving role; -8% to -12% below a clean lead-back price for Dobbins, on his injury history

Two-sidedWRLVAug 29

Klint Kubiak already turned play‑action shots off the zone run into a superstar receiver once, and Raiders camp is already showing who gets that role this time

Kubiak called plays for Minnesota in 2021, running the same wide‑zone rushing scheme his family has run for three generations, and it did more than feed the running back. Dalvin Cook’s heavy workload set up play‑action off nearly identical run looks, and Justin Jefferson turned that look into 1,616 yards in just his second season. Adam Thielen finished the same year over 1,000 yards too. The runner draws the safety down. The receiver who wins the play‑action shots is where the rest of the value goes.

Las Vegas is already sorting out which receiver that is. Tre Tucker played nine of the Raiders’ ten first‑team snaps in the preseason opener against Arizona, more than any other receiver in Kubiak’s offense, and got the early targets on the opening drive. Jalen Nailor is also starting, but the lead role and the play‑action shots are going to Tucker first.

Who it moves: Up — Tre Tucker (LV, WR), still priced like a committee piece even though camp has already named him the first read. Down — Jalen Nailor (LV, WR), who keeps a starting job but loses the shots that made Jefferson’s early seasons special.

Projected effect: +8% to +13% above a committee-receiver price for Tucker; -5% to -9% below a full-starter price for Nailor

Two-sidedRBNEAug 29

Mike Vrabel rode Derrick Henry as a true bell‑cow for five years in Tennessee, and New England’s backfield timeshare is already tilting toward one back

Vrabel does not manage a good running back’s workload down once he has one. Derrick Henry led the NFL in rushing in both 2019 and 2020 under him, clearing 300 carries both years and topping 2,000 yards in 2020, and he stayed the featured back through 2023 even as the passing game struggled around him. Whichever back proves he deserves the ball keeps getting it in a Vrabel offense, without a timeshare stepping back in to protect the other player’s workload.

New England has been splitting carries between TreVeyon Henderson and Rhamondre Stevenson, but Henderson’s snap share has already started climbing late in camp. That is the same kind of shift that preceded Henry taking the Titans’ backfield over outright, and it is exactly the signal a Vrabel offense tends to act on rather than smooth over.

Who it moves: Up — TreVeyon Henderson (NE, RB), still priced like one half of an even timeshare while camp reports already show him pulling ahead. Down — Rhamondre Stevenson (NE, RB), priced like a true co‑starter in a backfield that Vrabel’s own history says will not stay split much longer.

Projected effect: +8% to +14% above a 50/50 timeshare price for Henderson; -8% to -12% below a co-starter price for Stevenson

UnderpricedWRKCAug 29

Andy Reid has always built one receiver to run past everybody, and this year the job is Xavier Worthy’s alone

Reid has done this since his first Philadelphia rosters. Rookie DeSean Jackson caught only 62 passes in 2008 but turned them into 912 yards, averaging nearly 15 yards every time he touched the ball in a role built purely to stretch the field. Reid rebuilt the same part in Kansas City around Tyreek Hill starting in 2016, and Hill’s vertical role became the engine of the league’s scariest passing game for half a decade. The names change. Who it moves: Xavier Worthy (KC, WR). He is priced like a No. 2 receiver waiting for volume. Reid’s own history says the field‑stretcher gets fed the role the moment the other option is off the field, and Rice’s suspension hands it to him for the whole first month.

OverpricedRBMIAAug 24

The coach who built De’Von Achane’s game left for the Chargers this offseason, and his price hasn’t caught up

A wide‑zone rushing scheme is what turned Achane into an explosive part‑time back in 2023, averaging close to eight yards a carry, and the same scheme got a career year out of Raheem Mostert the season before. That coach spent four years running Miami’s offense before this year’s move to Los Angeles, where he now calls plays for the Chargers. The cutback lanes and manufactured yardage that made Achane special travel with the coach who designed them, not with the back who ran through them.

Everything else around Achane has also come apart. Miami signed Malik Willis at quarterback, lost both Tyreek Hill and Jaylen Waddle, and projects with one of the league’s weakest offensive lines. Defenses no longer have to respect the deep pass, so boxes get heavier and the cutback lanes that made Achane’s per‑carry numbers special shrink right along with them — the exact mechanism the scheme used to be built to beat.

Who it moves: De’Von Achane (MIA, RB). His price still trades on three years of scheme‑built efficiency. The coach who built it is running a different backfield now.

Projected effect: -6% to -11% below a 2025-usage price, mostly from yards per carry

UnderpricedWRSFAug 24

Kyle Shanahan invented the “wide back” role for Deebo Samuel once, and San Francisco’s roster says he is rebuilding it

No play‑caller in the league manufactures touches for one specific skill set the way Shanahan does. His clearest example is still Samuel’s own 2021 season: motion, jet sweeps and screens turned him into a player who racked up 1,770 total yards from scrimmage without ever being schemed like a conventional No.‑1 receiver running a route tree. The role was not about targets. It was about getting the ball into his hands in space by any means available.

San Francisco has since added Mike Evans and kept Christian McCaffrey, both of whom now sit ahead of Samuel in the target pecking order. That is precisely the setup that puts him back into the manufactured‑touch role instead of asking him to win as a true No.‑1, which is not his job in this room anymore. His 2025 line of 72 catches, 727 receiving yards and another 75 rushing already reads like the wide‑back role, not a featured receiver’s.

Who it moves: Deebo Samuel (SF, WR). He is priced like a fringe flex who needs target volume to matter. Shanahan’s own history says the role travels with him even at a low volume, in spike weeks the raw target count will not predict.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% above a standard flex price, concentrated in boom weeks

Since Bowles took over as Tampa Bay’s head coach in 2022, the Buccaneers have had a 1,000‑yard receiver every season, no matter who was calling plays or throwing the ball. Byron Leftwich ran the offense in 2022. Dave Canales took over in 2023. Liam Coen had it in 2024. Tom Brady retired and Baker Mayfield took over at quarterback in the middle of all that. The names around the job kept changing, and the job itself kept producing the same result anyway.

Mike Evans held that job through all of it, and now he is gone, traded to San Francisco. Egbuka steps into the opening as a second‑year receiver, and the numbers already treat him like the new No. 1: he projects for more catches, yards and touchdowns than any other Buccaneer, well ahead of a healthy Chris Godwin working from the slot.

Who it moves: Emeka Egbuka (TB, WR). The room is still pricing him like a No. 2 receiver finding his footing. Bowles’s track record says whoever holds that job in Tampa Bay gets fed like a true No. 1.

Projected effect: +7% to +12% above a No. 2 receiver price

UnderpricedRBLACAug 23

Mike McDaniel’s outside‑zone scheme keeps turning ordinary backs into monster rushers, and Omarion Hampton has no committee to share it with

In Miami, McDaniel’s wide‑zone rushing scheme produced huge, efficient seasons out of backs nobody expected it from. Raheem Mostert led the league in yards per carry in 2022 running behind it. Who it moves: Omarion Hampton (LAC, RB). He is priced like a solid early‑down back in his second year. A zone scheme that has already made two different backs into league leaders in efficiency, with no real competition for his touches, is worth more than that.

Projected effect: +6% to +11% above his price, mostly from yards per carry

Two-sidedRBARIAug 23

Down — Jeremiyah Love (ARI, RB), still priced like a locked‑in Week 1 workhorse despite a coach who has already shown he won’t wait around when a back’s timeline is unclear.

Projected effect: +10% to +16% above a handcuff price for Conner in the short term; -8% to -12% off a locked-in workhorse price for Love until his timeline clears

UnderpricedQBINDAug 22

Shane Steichen’s offense manufactures quarterback rushing touchdowns, and Daniel Jones is the newest one to get them

Steichen called plays for Jalen Hurts in Philadelphia in 2021 and 2022, building the league’s most productive quarterback run game around him. Hurts scored 13 rushing touchdowns in that 2022 MVP‑runner‑up season, most of them on designed runs and short‑yardage keepers rather than scrambles. Steichen brought that same identity to Indianapolis as head coach in 2023, and it survived the switch from Anthony Richardson’s legs to Daniel Jones’s.

Jones was never miscast as a pocket passer in New York, but the Giants stopped using his legs as anything more than an escape valve. Steichen’s offense does not have that option. Designed quarterback runs and goal‑line keepers are a feature of the system, not an emergency plan, and the system does not care who is taking the snap.

Who it moves: Daniel Jones (IND, QB). The market is still pricing him off his passing line, the same way it always has. Steichen’s system adds rushing touchdowns to that price for free.

Projected effect: +9% to +14% above a passing‑only price

UnderpricedRBNYJAug 22

Defensive head coaches play it safe on offense, and Aaron Glenn’s Jets already look the part

A coach’s background is one of the most reliable shortcuts in football. Coaches who came up on defense tend to build offenses that protect field position and shorten the game — more called runs on early downs, more clock burned, fewer total possessions. Bill Belichick’s Patriots and John Harbaugh’s Ravens both ran this identity for over a decade, leaning on the running back long before either team had a great one at the position.

Glenn, a career defensive coach hired to run the Jets in 2025, fits the mold, and the 2026 numbers already show it before a snap is played: Geno Smith’s projected passing volume sits near the bottom of the league among full‑time starters, exactly what a program built to protect its defense produces.

Who it moves: Breece Hall (NYJ, RB). He is priced like a complementary back in an offense that still throws plenty. A staff built to shorten games leans on its lead back more than the raw pass‑attempt numbers suggest.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% above his price

UnderpricedRBDETAug 22

Dan Campbell never stopped daring it on fourth down, and that always creates a market for a short‑yardage back

No coach in the league goes for it on fourth down more often than Dan Campbell, and that is not a personality quirk — it is dozens of extra offensive snaps a season inside plus territory that a cautious coach would have punted or kicked away instead. Those plays cluster in exactly the situations where a physical, downhill runner earns his keep: third‑and‑short, fourth‑and‑one, the goal line. Detroit has leaned on a designated short‑yardage piece for that work rather than asking its lead back to do all of it, which is part of why the Lions’ rushing touchdowns have never belonged to one player the way the yardage does.

Isiah Pacheco arrives from Kansas City into exactly that kind of role, behind Jahmyr Gibbs rather than ahead of him. He does not need three‑down volume to matter in this offense. He needs the ball inside the five, and Campbell’s fourth‑down habit means he will see more of those snaps than a typical backup would.

Who it moves: Isiah Pacheco (DET, RB). He is priced as pure injury insurance. Campbell’s fourth‑down aggressiveness turns him into a real source of rushing touchdowns even with the starter healthy.

Projected effect: +10% to +18% above a handcuff‑only price, most of it in touchdowns rather than yards

Projected effect: +8% to +14% above his current price

OverpricedRBPITAug 20

Mike McCarthy has not built a workhorse backfield in almost two decades, and Pittsburgh is about to prove it again

Thirteen years in Green Bay produced exactly one true workhorse season: Eddie Lacy in 2013. On either side of it were committees — Ryan Grant and Brandon Jackson, then Ty Montgomery, then Aaron Jones splitting touches with Jamaal Williams even after Jones proved he was a top‑12 back. Dallas told the same story from the other end. Ezekiel Elliott was already a workhorse when McCarthy got there, and by 2021 and 2022 Tony Pollard had played well enough to force a real timeshare anyway. The thread is a coach who would rather leave touches on the table than run one back into the ground.

Pittsburgh signed Rico Dowdle for early‑down and goal‑line work, and is running him next to passing‑down back Jaylen Warren. The staff is already describing a real two‑man plan instead of a clear starter. That is the McCarthy pattern showing up right on schedule.

Who it moves: Jaylen Warren (PIT, RB) and Rico Dowdle (PIT, RB). Neither is priced like a committee back yet, and both should be. Warren catches passes, which makes him the safer of the two discounts in PPR.

Projected effect: -8% to -14% below a workhorse price, for both backs

UnderpricedQBCARAug 20

Dave Canales has rebuilt one quarterback’s career already, and Bryce Young is the second

Canales left Tampa Bay after the 2023 season, having helped turn Baker Mayfield’s career around. That was a Pro Bowl year for a quarterback who had bounced through three teams in the four seasons before it. No coach deserves all the credit, but the timing points at him as much as at the player. Carolina hired Canales in 2024 to do the same job for Bryce Young, and the season followed the arc that pattern predicts: benched in the first month, back in the lineup by midseason, and clearly playing better by the end. The Panthers have not looked at the position since.

A full second camp in the same system, with that benching a year behind him, is exactly when a Canales quarterback is supposed to take the next step.

Who it moves: Bryce Young (CAR, QB). He is still priced like a former bust, not like a passer whose own coach has already rebuilt a career from worse.

Projected effect: +10% to +16% above his price

UnderpricedQBJAXAug 20

Liam Coen’s one year calling plays gave Baker Mayfield the best season of his career — now he has Trevor Lawrence

Coen took over Tampa Bay’s offense in 2024, a year after Canales’s revival season with Mayfield, and Mayfield got better again. He set a career high in touchdown passes in an offense built on play‑action and quick, high‑percentage throws. That scheme turned an up‑and‑down veteran into one of the most efficient passers in the league, and it got Coen the Jaguars head job in 2025.

Lawrence is a different project. He was the No. 1 overall pick, and his physical talent has never been the question. His efficiency has. A scheme that manufactured easy completions for a journeyman may fit even better on a talented passer who mainly needs the offense around him to ask less of him on early downs, not more.

Who it moves: Trevor Lawrence (JAX, QB). The market remembers four up‑and‑down years under three different play‑callers. It has not yet priced in the coach who just did this exact job for someone else.

Projected effect: +8% to +13% above his price

UnderpricedRBLVAug 19

The zone‑scheme coaching tree has been making running backs for thirty years, and Klint Kubiak just brought it to Las Vegas

No coaching family has a longer paper trail than this one. Mike Shanahan’s Denver turned a sixth‑round pick into Terrell Davis, then did it again with Mike Anderson, then again with Olandis Gary and Reuben Droughns. Gary Kubiak took the same outside‑zone system to Houston and got a 1,600‑yard season out of undrafted Arian Foster. Kyle Shanahan has done it in Atlanta and San Francisco with backs the league did not want. The runner is not what stays the same. The scheme is. Wide zone with a cutback lane creates yards the back did not have to earn.

Klint Kubiak — Gary’s son, and the third generation running this playbook — now calls it for the Raiders. The family reputation cuts both ways, and it is only fair to say so. This is also the tree that invented the committee. A scheme that makes any back productive has no reason to protect one back’s workload. That is the risk you are taking on.

Who it moves: Ashton Jeanty (LV, RB). The usual knock on a back in this scheme is that anyone could do the job. Jeanty flips that: a real first‑round talent inside the system, instead of a system inventing a back out of nothing. You get the scheme’s efficiency and a player good enough to keep the job.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% above his price, with committee risk as the stated downside

UnderpricedRBKCAug 19

Andy Reid’s lead back keeps finishing better than his price, and this year that back is Kenneth Walker

Twenty‑five years, two teams, one pattern: Duce Staley, Brian Westbrook, LeSean McCoy, Jamaal Charles, Kareem Hunt, Clyde Edwards‑Helaire as a rookie, Isiah Pacheco. Reid’s lead back finishes in the top 12 far more often than his summer price suggests. Volume is not the reason — Reid has never been a 300‑carry coach. Field position and light boxes are. An offense this dangerous through the air faces fewer defenders in the box and starts drives closer to the end zone, so the carries a Reid back does get are worth more than the same carries somewhere else.

The 2026 change makes the pattern stronger, not weaker. Kansas City is no longer the pure passing offense it was. There is more quarterback running, more efficient early‑down running, and a small cap on raw pass attempts. That is the exact setup in which this pattern has paid before.

Who it moves: Kenneth Walker III (KC, RB). He was already one of the biggest winners of any team change on the board. The coach is the second half of that argument, and the room usually pays for the new team without paying for the play‑caller.

Projected effect: +10% to +15% above his price

UnderpricedTECLEAug 19

Todd Monken feeds the tight end everywhere he goes, and Cleveland just hired him

Play‑callers have position habits, and Monken’s is the easiest to read in football: the tight end is a first option, not a checkdown. At Georgia the tight end was the offense. In Baltimore he ran a 60% run‑action rate, used two‑back sets as often as almost anyone, and still finished first in explosive pass rate and air yards per attempt. The tight ends ate through all of it. An offense that lines up heavy and still throws deep is the rarest and friendliest setup a tight end can get.

Cleveland brought in the whole blueprint — Monken and Travis Switzer, from that same Baltimore staff. A scheme change resets who gets the targets, and the market is slow to believe a pecking order it has not seen yet.

Who it moves: Harold Fannin Jr. (CLE, TE). A projection of about 78 catches and 816 yards is No. 1 receiver treatment, not leftover tight‑end scraps. He is priced like the scraps.

Projected effect: +12% to +18% above his price

Two-sidedTE / WRNYGAug 19

John Harbaugh and Matt Nagy will play heavy in New York, and that costs the wide receivers targets

Which players a coach puts on the field is the least exciting signal there is, and one of the most reliable, because it is arithmetic rather than opinion. Harbaugh’s teams have always lived in two‑tight‑end and two‑back sets, and league scheme analysis expects fewer three‑receiver sets in New York under Harbaugh and Nagy. Fewer three‑receiver snaps means fewer routes to split among wideouts. The tight ends and backs pick up the routes they lose.

The roster says the plan out loud instead of leaving it to be guessed at: the Giants added Isaiah Likely and fullback Patrick Ricard. Teams do not sign a fullback to run three‑receiver sets.

Who it moves: Up — Isaiah Likely (NYG, TE), who lands in exactly the role his new coaches like to use. Down — the second‑tier receivers, Darius Slayton and Darnell Mooney, who lose snaps rather than talent. Malik Nabers is mostly safe: a true No. 1 gets his targets no matter who else is on the field, which is why the players behind him take the hit.

Projected effect: +8% to +12% for the tight end, -10% to -15% for the second-tier receivers

Two-sidedWRSFAug 19

Some play-callers answer every coverage by throwing to the same man — and San Francisco is built that way now

The classic example is Scott Linehan. His top receivers were monsters — Calvin Johnson’s 1,964‑yard season, Dez Bryant’s three straight years of 1,200 yards and 12 touchdowns — and almost nobody else in those rooms was worth starting. That was not luck with talent. It is what happens when a play‑caller answers every coverage by throwing to the same man, and it makes the most lopsided receiver rooms in the sport.

You do not need Linehan on the sideline to draft the pattern. You need a room shaped like his, and San Francisco has one. After Mike Evans arrived, the 49ers became the most top‑heavy receiver group in football, with George Kittle and Christian McCaffrey taking the middle of the field and a long drop to the next wideout.

Who it moves: Up — Mike Evans (SF, WR), the man they throw to. Down — everyone behind him. The other 49ers receivers are best‑ball dart throws, not weekly starters, and how uneven the targets are week to week is the reason.

Projected effect: +5% to +9% for the No. 1 receiver; treat the No. 2 and No. 3 as format calls, not bargains

UnderpricedRBDETAug 19

Drew Petzing throws to his running backs, and Detroit handed him the best one in football

Catches are the most underpriced thing a running back can do. Carries depend on the score, the weather and the defense in front of him. Targets to a back are called plays. A coordinator who calls them is handing you the steadiest fantasy points on the field, and drafters still pay for rushing yards instead.

Petzing comes to Detroit from an Arizona offense that threw to its backs often. He inherits a declared workhorse: Dan Campbell called Jahmyr Gibbs the team’s every‑down back for 2026, David Montgomery was traded to Houston, and Isiah Pacheco is a backup rather than a committee partner.

Who it moves: Jahmyr Gibbs (DET, RB). Watch your format here. This edge comes from catches, so it is worth much more in full PPR than in standard, and more again in leagues that pay a bonus on running‑back catches.

Projected effect: +4% to +8% above his price in full PPR

Two-sidedWR / RBCHIAug 19

The best proof a coach matters is what happens after he leaves — Ben Johnson shows both halves

Most claims about coaching cannot be proved either way, which is why this one is worth more than most: you can watch the same offense with him and without him. Detroit’s rushing success rate fell after Ben Johnson left, and losses like that pile up instead of evening out. Worse down and distance means fewer trips to the goal line, and touchdowns are where an inside runner’s fantasy value lives.

Meanwhile, the offense he took over in Chicago is being rebuilt in his image: run‑first, heavy on play‑action, and with the targets going to fewer players now that DJ Moore and Olamide Zaccheaus are both gone. That pair walked out with 166 targets between them, and they had spent most of 2025 lined up ahead of the receiver who now inherits them.

Who it moves: Up — Luther Burden III (CHI, WR), the most logical player to absorb those vacated targets after a rookie year that put him top five among receivers in catch rate (77%), yards per target (10.8) and yards per route run (2.9). Buy him at a mid‑tier receiver price and plan for boom‑or‑bust weeks, because a run‑first offense makes the week‑to‑week swings wider. Down — David Montgomery (HOU, RB), who fades because of efficiency, not because he loses carries.

Projected effect: +6% to +10% for the receiver, -4% to -8% for the back
